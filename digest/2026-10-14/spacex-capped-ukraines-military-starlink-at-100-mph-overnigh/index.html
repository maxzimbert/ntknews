SpaceX Capped Ukraine's Military Starlink at 100 mph Overnight, With No Warning. Kyiv Lost Missions and Missile Warnings the Same Night.
SpaceX limited Ukraine's military Starlink as Russian missiles left 600,000 without power.
Truths — What Happened

A Speed Limit Nobody Announced Hit Ukraine's Drones in the Middle of the Night

Ukrainian forces learned about the change the way any customer would. Their connections kept dropping, so they called SpaceX support, which told them Starshield service would be restricted on platforms moving at 100 mph or faster (Fox News). Kyiv says its service was contracted through the U.S. government until May 15, 2027, and that the limit never applied to its terminals before (Fox News).

The damage showed up fast. Ukraine's 7th Rapid Response Corps said one drone unit on the Huliaipole front canceled missions, and it reported dropouts even below the stated threshold (Fox News). A unit representative told Reuters the connection could drop for 10 to 20 seconds at just 110 to 120 km/h, roughly 70 mph (Deutsche Welle). Serhii Beskrestnov, Zelensky's defense-technology adviser, said the limit takes away "one of our few chances to defend the country" against Russia's jet-powered Shahed drones, which can fly up to 370 mph (Fox News) (AFP).

SpaceX had not answered Ukraine's request to reverse the change as of Wednesday morning (Fox News). Nobody has confirmed that Musk ordered it, and Fox notes that a Pentagon contract alone does not show who holds authority over a restriction like this (Fox News).

The Same Night, the Warnings Stopped Too

The speed limit was not the only thing that failed Ukraine that night. Ballistic missiles hit Kyiv for a second straight night before sirens could sound, and Zelensky said no partner gave advance warning (Los Angeles Times). Russia used 39 ballistic missiles and more than 120 drones, and 600,000 people lost power (Daily Beast).

Washington's explanation was the weather. U.S. officials told Ukrainians the equipment that detects Russian launches failed because of clouds, according to CBS sources, and Ukrainian officials said they did not buy it (The New Republic). Ukraine's air force spokesman said the first wave came without warning, though Kyiv was alerted to three later waves (Los Angeles Times). Trump said Tuesday, "No, we haven't," when asked whether intelligence sharing had stopped (The New Republic).

It's obvious they (the United States) are helping Russia for some reason.

Senior Ukrainian official, speaking to AFP

Why Kyiv Reads This as Punishment

Kyiv's suspicion has a timeline behind it. On Friday Trump lifted sanctions on Russian diesel and announced a plan to import $4.5 billion of it (Daily Beast). Zelensky called the deal weakness. A multiday pressure campaign followed, in which U.S. officials threatened to cut intelligence and weapons deliveries unless Ukraine stopped hitting Russian refineries (The New Republic). On Saturday Trump said Ukraine needed a new president, and blamed Zelensky for diesel prices (Los Angeles Times). Most experts put the real cause of high fuel prices on the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran and the closed Strait of Hormuz (AFP).

Musk weighed in that same Saturday: "Zelensky has gone too far" (The New Republic). In 2025 he promised Starlink would never be used "as a bargaining chip" (Al Jazeera).

Poland pays for more than 2,900 of the terminals, and its foreign minister, Radoslaw Sikorski, put the question publicly: "are you playing politics with your products again?" (Fox News). Kyiv has asked the U.S., SpaceX and NATO for an explanation ([The Independent](https://www.independent.co

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The next two weeks will answer who actually holds the switch.

SpaceX Will Almost Certainly Restore Most of the Service Within Days, and the Explanation Will Name No One

Ukraine, Poland and NATO have all asked for answers, and SpaceX has stayed silent. The most likely outcome is a quiet partial rollback, with a statement that calls the limit a configuration or compliance matter. That statement will not say who asked for it. The company has a clean way out: Fox notes that nobody has confirmed Musk ordered the change, and that a Pentagon contract does not settle who controls operational limits (Fox News). Whoever owns the decision gets to stay vague about it.

The bigger problem for SpaceX is Poland. Warsaw pays for the terminals, and Sikorski has already told Musk, in public, that European customers are watching (Al Jazeera). A government that funds a service can ask for guarantees in writing, and it will. Expect Warsaw and other European buyers to press for contract language that bars unilateral limits. That would be a first for a company that has never had to promise anything of the kind.

Ukraine Will Speed Up Its Search for a Starlink Replacement, and Europe Will Pay for It

Zelensky said in August that Kyiv was looking to build an alternative to Starlink (Deutsche Welle). Even if SpaceX reverses course this week, a few hours of dropped missions has turned a long-term preference into an urgent order. The likeliest venue is London. Zelensky meets Prime Minister Andy Burnham on Wednesday, and Burnham has asked for military and financial support plans to be ready "for the coming weeks" (The Independent). A pledge of money for European satellite links, or for ground-based relays that fliers can use below the speed threshold, fits what Burnham has already said he wants.

The winners are whichever European suppliers can show a working product before winter. The loser is the arrangement that made Ukraine's front line dependent on one American billionaire's goodwill. The timing is brutal, because any replacement will take months and the winter strikes on the grid will not wait.

Russia Will Keep Hitting the Grid and Test How Far Washington Lets It Go

Moscow has read the week the way Zelensky read it. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia was "carefully noting" any shift in American backing (AFP). The most likely next step is another large ballistic strike on energy sites within days, because Zelensky's own intelligence says more launches are being prepared (ABC Australia). Each strike that lands without a loud American response tells the Kremlin the energy ceasefire exists only in Trump's announcements.

Kirill Dmitriev has said the first diesel contracts with the United States will be signed in the coming days (AFP). When they are, Russia will hold a signed deal with Washington while its missiles keep falling on Kyiv. Trump faces midterms next month and needs lower fuel prices. Every outage in Ukraine that shows the ceasefire failing makes his central claim harder to sell.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The doors that just opened are narrower than the ones Truths and Probabilities covered, and they all start with a terminal Iran seized in a mountain hideout.

A Starlink Terminal Found in an Iranian Militant Camp Gives Washington a Reason to Pull the Whitelist

Start with a detail that sat at the bottom of an Al Jazeera report on attacks near Iran's Chabahar port. The Revolutionary Guard said it killed at least two suspected attackers near the port and recovered a Starlink device along with a large quantity of weapons (Al Jazeera). Chabahar is where U.S. forces struck infrastructure and naval control facilities in July, and Tehran says outside powers fund the groups hitting its security forces. Last year's precedent matters here: SpaceX whitelisted terminals in Ukraine in February, cutting off Russian military users (Wikipedia). Until this week, SpaceX had no public excuse to tighten that control on Ukraine's own fleet. Now it has two: a speed cap that fits a "misuse" story, and a terminal in a militant hideout that suggests Starlink hardware is leaking into conflicts nobody contracted for. Neither is evidence of Ukrainian diversion. But a company that wants to explain a throttle without naming a political order now has a compliance narrative sitting in the open. The winner is whoever needs the limit to look technical. The loser is any Ukrainian unit that fits the profile.

Bulgaria's Burned Antennas Could Make the Ground Stations the Next Hostage

That compliance story runs into a second problem. A Bulgarian opposition lawmaker said two masked men set fire to 11 of 17 SpaceX antennas at a secret facility near Sofia, and Prime Minister Rumen Radev, who confirmed an investigation, neither confirmed nor denied it (ANSA). If true, the throttle is no longer the only way to hurt Ukraine's links. Ground stations in NATO countries are physical, fixed and findable. That makes the second door visible only from inside the first: once SpaceX is seen restricting service, anyone who wants to restrict it further has a target list. Poland pays for more than 2,900 terminals, so Warsaw now has a reason to ask where the relays sit and who guards them.

Poland Pays for the Terminals. This Week Gave It a Reason to Pay for the Switch.

Here is the generative door. Probabilities covered Warsaw demanding contract language. The newly reachable step is bigger: a European buyer group that owns, or co-controls, the ground infrastructure serving Ukraine. Poland has the money, Zelensky meets Burnham today, and Radev's investigation gives European security services a shared reason to harden the same sites. Combine a funder, a host country and a grievance, and a European-held relay layer becomes a procurement question instead of a slogan. The 2023 refusal to enable Starlink near Crimea showed what one owner's veto costs (Wikipedia). A switch held by buyers cannot be flipped by one man's mood. Building it takes months, and winter will not wait.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The accusation travels faster than the evidence, and several people have reasons to keep it moving.

"The U.S. Government Throttled Starlink and Muted the Missile Warnings to Help Russia"

Start with what Kyiv's own officials actually said. A senior Ukrainian official told AFP that Washington was "obviously" helping Russia, and Serhii Beskrestnov told his audience the speed cap "cannot be a coincidence" (AFP). The New Republic went further, opening its Starlink item with the question of whether Musk was kneecapping access "on behalf of President Donald Trump" (The New Republic). The story that results has three parts: Trump ordered it, Musk carried it out, and the clouds were a cover.

The belief makes sense. A president who lifted sanctions on Russian diesel on Friday and called for Zelensky's replacement on Saturday has given people a motive, and a sudden cap with no warning looks like a message. Fear of abandonment is a reasonable thing to feel in Kyiv this week, and nobody should be mocked for feeling it.

Now sort the claims by what supports them. The senior official gave no evidence for his statement, and Beskrestnov said "without elaborating" when he tied the limit to the political situation (Los Angeles Times). Fox reports that nobody has confirmed Musk personally ordered the change, and that a Pentagon contract alone does not show who holds authority over a restriction like this (Fox News). A Ukrainian lawmaker on the Defense Committee told the Associated Press that intelligence sharing has not stopped, while the air force spokesman said Kyiv did get warnings for three later waves (Los Angeles Times). Even the Ukrainian defense ministry source told the BBC the "root cause" was still being investigated (BBC). The ministry's own public statement blamed SpaceX, and named no one in Washington.

Here the claim splits. That SpaceX acted without warning is documented. That the U.S. government ordered it, or ordered a blackout of missile warnings, rests on inference from timing. Two separate failures arriving the same night can share a cause, and they can also share only a calendar.

The cost of treating the inference as fact is concrete. Every Ukrainian official who says "helping Russia" on the record hands Moscow a clip of an ally in open quarrel, and Peskov has already said the Kremlin is watching for exactly that (AFP). The accusation also lets everyone with a documented responsibility off lightly. SpaceX can answer a charge about Trump's motives and say nothing about why it gave no notice. Washington can deny a conspiracy and skip the question of why its missile detection went dark for one night and worked at 3 a.m.

The honest version is harder to repeat and more damning of the people involved: a company changed a military customer's service with no notice, and an administration offered weather as its explanation. Neither needs a secret order to be a failure.
