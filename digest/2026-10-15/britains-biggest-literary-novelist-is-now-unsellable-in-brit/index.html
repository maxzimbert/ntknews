Britain's Biggest Literary Novelist Is Now Unsellable in Britain. Her 2.3 Million U.K. Copies Are the Collateral.
Faber pulls Sally Rooney's novels from U.K. sale over her Palestine Action pledge.
Truths — What Happened

A Publisher Chose Its Author's Politics Over Its Own Royalties. Sally Rooney told the public in August 2025 that she would use proceeds from her books and their TV adaptations to support Palestine Action, the direct-action group Britain banned as a terrorist organization a month earlier (AP). Faber, her publisher, then faced a problem it could not draft around: paying her royalties could mean funding a banned group. On Oct. 14 it stopped supplying her work in the U.K. "in any format," and said it acted with her agreement (BBC). Rooney had sold 2.3 million books in Britain, out of six million worldwide, according to NielsenIQ BookData (BBC). Faber will keep selling her elsewhere, and the withdrawal does not touch Australian sales (ABC).

Faber called selling a writer's work without paying her "an unconscionable situation for a publisher" (CBS News). The law never named her. It simply made her own announced plan the crime.

Bookshops Learned They Were Holding the Last Copies. The decision landed on people who had no say in it. E-book editions were already gone, and booksellers spent a day working out whether the paper copies in their stockrooms were legal to sell. The Booksellers Association confirmed with Faber that shops may sell existing stock but cannot reorder (AP). Waterstones got the same answer (BBC).

Lucy Burke, a bookseller at Camden Town Bookshop in London, said she tried to stock up before the supply closed, only to find the distributors had already run out. "Now it seems like what we have is all we're going to have," she said (AP). A bestselling writer's backlist is now a finite inventory, and the shop that sells the last copy has nothing to reorder.

The Ban Behind It Is Heading to the Supreme Court. Rooney's pledge only became a legal problem because of what Parliament did to Palestine Action in July 2025, after its activists broke into RAF Brize Norton and sprayed paint into two aircraft. Support for the group now carries up to 14 years in prison. More than 3,700 people have been arrested, and about 2,500 have been charged, though none has gone to trial (BBC). The courts have not agreed with each other. In February 2026, the High Court ruled the ban unlawful, and in June five Court of Appeal judges overruled it (ABC).

Next month the Supreme Court hears the appeal from co-founder Huda Ammori, and every prosecution under the ban is on hold until it rules (The Independent). Until then, 2,500 charged people and one novelist wait on the same ruling.

The government maintains the ban leaves lawful protest alone. The Home Office calls it "a fundamental democratic right" (AP). Campaign Against Antisemitism, which says it reported Rooney to counterterrorism police, told CBS she "can't have her cake and eat it" (CBS News).

Rooney has cancelled her UK travel for fear of arrest (ABC). Her books can still be bought in Dublin, Sydney and New York, and cannot be bought in London.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The Supreme Court hearing is the only date in this story that matters, and the shelf will follow it.

The Supreme Court Will Not Settle the Rooney Question Next Month. The justices hear Huda Ammori's appeal in November, and a ruling almost certainly arrives after that, not during it. Courts weighing a terrorism designation with this many prior reversals rarely rule from the bench. That leaves Faber's decision in place through the holidays, the biggest book-buying stretch of the year. Bookshops will sell down the last paper copies of "Normal People" on a countdown nobody can adjust, and the stock that can't be reordered becomes the product. Expect resale listings for Rooney's hardbacks to climb well past cover price before Christmas, and expect Waterstones and independents to quietly move remaining copies to the front table, because a vanishing title sells itself. The winners are the shops holding inventory. The loser is every bookseller who sold through early, and Faber, which gave up the UK's single steadiest literary backlist to avoid a prosecutor.

Faber's Precedent Will Reach Other Publishers Before the Court Does. A publisher's legal team now has a template: if a writer announces money for a proscribed group, the contract becomes a risk, and the book can go. The most likely next step is not another Rooney. It is a quiet round of contract reviews at UK houses, with lawyers asking whether authors, translators and even charity-linked royalty clauses touch any of the 80-odd groups on the proscribed list. Most of those reviews will end in nothing, and nobody will announce them. But a few will end in an author being asked to sign a declaration, and the first one to refuse in public will do so within weeks, likely a writer with an Irish or overseas passport and nothing to lose in London.

I have made every effort to be honest with the public about my beliefs and intentions, and I am wholly prepared to accept the consequences.

Sally Rooney, novelist

Her statement hands every other writer a script. The cost of saying it lands on publishers, who carry the criminal exposure while the author carries only the principle.

Whichever Way the Court Rules, the Charged Cases Come Back at Once. All prosecutions under the ban are frozen until the ruling. If the Supreme Court upholds the designation, the Crown Prosecution Service gets a green light on about 2,500 charged people, the large majority of them holding signs, and the average arrestee is 59 years old. Prosecutors will then face the first real trials of grandparents under the Terrorism Act, with juries deciding whether a placard is a crime. If the court strikes the ban down, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood loses the flagship of her counterterrorism record and must decide whether to re-legislate. Either outcome puts the government on the defensive, and Rooney's absent novels will be the exhibit critics point to. The first sign comes early: expect the Crown Prosecution Service to say within days of the hearing that it is preparing for both results, and expect no one to say how many of those 2,500 cases it still wants.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The doors that opened this week lead through the people who sign the cheques, not the people who write the books.

Rooney's Withdrawal Gives a Rival Publisher a Test Case Nobody Else Can Afford to Run. Faber chose to stop paying and stop selling. Another route now exists that did not before this week: a publisher or the author's own company keeps the royalties in a UK account, pays nothing to Palestine Action, and asks a court or the Crown Prosecution Service whether withholding is enough. Faber's statement said it cannot pass on earnings, which leaves a gap between the money sitting in the UK and the money reaching the group. Rooney's pledge fused the two. Her Irish residence, her Irish tax base and a UK ledger make a cleaner experiment than any British author could offer. If a declaratory ruling on that split happened, Faber would have a path back to Waterstones' front tables, and the Home Office would have to say in public whether it prosecutes a pledge or only a payment. The Supreme Court hearing next month gives that question a courtroom to land in, since the justices will be reading the proscription's reach anyway. The catch is that someone has to volunteer to be the defendant, and Faber has already shown it won't.

If That Question Gets Asked, the 2,500 Charged Cases Get a Yardstick. This one depends entirely on the first. Prosecutors have charged people for holding signs, which is speech. A ruling that the offence turns on money moving, rather than on a declaration of belief, would hand defence lawyers a line to test every pending case against: did this person fund the group, or only say they back it? The Crown Prosecution Service would then have to sort its caseload by that line, and some of the largest groups of defendants, the sign-holders, would sit on the wrong side of it. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood's distinction between supporting the Palestinian cause and supporting Palestine Action stops being a talking point and becomes a charging standard. That is the generative door in this section. A novelist's awkward ledger, the most unwelcome possible test case, could force the law to say plainly what it prohibits, and a statute that says plainly what it prohibits is one fewer 59-year-old arrested for a placard.

Booksellers Hold the Only Leverage Neither Side Has Used. The Booksellers Association sits between Faber, the Home Office and thousands of shops. It has already extracted one answer this week: stock on shelves can be sold. It can now ask the next question, in writing, as a trade body: what exactly makes a sale of the last copy lawful when the original supply is not? That inquiry costs nothing, requires no defendant, and puts the Home Office's reading of its own statute on the record before the court rules. If the answer is vague, the trade has a public example of a law it cannot comply with. If the answer is specific, the Faber question above gets easier to bring.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The loudest claims about Rooney's shelf come from people who need the shelf to mean something else.

"Sally Rooney Can't Have Her Cake and Eat It."

The line comes from a spokesperson for Campaign Against Antisemitism, which told CBS News that Rooney is "finding out that she can't have her cake and eat it" (CBS News). The framing is that she wanted a bestselling career in Britain and a vow to fund a banned group, and that the law has merely sent the bill. It answers a real fear. People who watched antisemitic incidents rise during the Gaza war want someone held to account, and a famous name makes the accountability visible.

Look at what the sentence assumes. It assumes a trade: Rooney chose her principle, so the loss is her own. But the bill did not land on her alone. Faber lost a backlist, and booksellers lost reorders on a title they never touched. Readers lost the book. The statement also treats the pledge as the offense, when her pledge was a sentence in an op-ed and nothing had yet moved from a ledger to the group. The group says it reported her to counterterrorism police. A report to police is a request for punishment, and reporting a novelist for announcing an intention is a different act from stopping a payment.

The benefit is plain enough. Each time "cake" is repeated, a legal question about when a statement of belief becomes a financial crime gets converted into a story about a hypocrite. A hypocrite has no remaining claim on anyone's attention, and the group that coined the line gets a win without a court ever deciding whether it was right. Nobody has to defend a charging standard when the target has already been judged unlikable.

The phrase also quietly erases the person it pretends to describe. Rooney did not hide the cost. She said she was "wholly prepared to accept the consequences," and cancelled her own UK travel for fear of arrest, which is the opposite of wanting it both ways. Someone who announces a plan, loses a market, and accepts the loss in public has not tried to eat anything she was not already paying for. The line works only on a reader who has not seen her statement.

The test of whether this is honest argument or smear is simple. Ask the same question about a different pledge. If a British author announced royalties for any other proscribed group, nobody would say she wanted both. They would say she was admitting to a crime. The cake line does that work in disguise: it convicts without naming a statute, and it keeps the debate away from the one thing the Supreme Court is about to read.

There is an older version of this move. When governments in the 1950s pressed publishers and studios to drop writers over their affiliations, the pressure came with a moral phrase attached, never a legal one, because the legal case was thin. The vocabulary of fairness did what the statute could not. The shelf emptied first, and the argument about why came later, if at all.
