Chicago Spent $9.4 Billion Redeveloping the City. The South and West Sides Got $55 Million of It.
A university study found Chicago's tax-increment financing program favored downtown for four decades.
Truths — What Happened

A University of Illinois Chicago study put a number on something South and West Side residents have said for years. Researchers spent more than a year building a dataset from public records on tax increment financing, the program Chicago has used more aggressively than any other major American city since 1984 (The New York Times). The mechanism is simple on paper: freeze property values in a district, capture the extra tax revenue as values rise, and reinvest it locally. In practice, five downtown-adjacent neighborhoods got $1.6 billion of the $2.6 billion in private-project TIF funding tracked since the program began, while five South and West Side communities got $55 million combined, barely half what the lowest-funded downtown neighborhood received.

98 percent
the share of property value gains inside TIF districts the study says would have happened anyway, without the subsidy.

Juan González, the researcher who led the study, said the program "morphed into a runaway development tool with little accountability to the public."

Instead of reducing the city's historic economic inequities, it has exacerbated them.

Juan González, Great Cities Institute

That question, who gets to see where the money goes, is the same one now splitting the Chicago school board. Board member Jennifer Custer withdrew Thursday from the race to become the district's first elected board president, saying she wouldn't put her family through a fight against candidates she expects will be backed by millions in outside spending (Chalkbeat). Custer had built a record as the board's most consistent dissenter, the lone no vote on hiring CEO Macquline King, the lone no vote on two revenue referendums pushed by Mayor Brandon Johnson's allies, and one of seven members who opposed a budget that leaned on $150 million in state money that hadn't been approved yet. That money is still missing. CPS rehired more than 300 of the 700 employees it laid off last month, but the district's $10 billion budget still depends on Springfield delivering funds Illinois lawmakers haven't passed (CBS News). King has told legislators directly that without it, CPS faces "disruptive mid-year cuts to our classrooms."

Two thousand miles away, Texas has already decided what happens when a district's numbers don't add up: someone in Austin loses their elected seat. State law lets Education Commissioner Mike Morath replace elected trustees with his own appointees after five straight years of failing grades at a single campus, a power he's used seven times since 2023, including ousting Fort Worth's superintendent even after parents credited her with academic gains (The 74). Austin's board is next in line.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

Springfield's veto session doesn't start until October, but the countdown that matters is measured in payrolls, not calendar months.

CPS Will Miss Another Payroll Deadline Before Lawmakers Vote on the $150 Million. The district has already told legislators it solved this month's payroll puzzle, but that was a one-time fix, not a structural one, and the next payroll cycle lands before the fall veto session even convenes (CBS News). The most likely outcome is a second round of rehire-then-recut whiplash for CPS staff sometime in September, because Speaker Chris Welch has committed to a vote in the fall session, not before it, and Gov. JB Pritzker has made clear he won't move money that isn't in a plan yet. Watch whether Macquline King goes back to the board with another interim patch, probably another short-term loan or a reserve draw, in the next two weeks. Each patch buys CPS a few weeks and costs the district leverage, because it lets Springfield delay without consequence.

That delay has a second effect nobody in Springfield has to answer for directly: it hollows out the field for CPS board president. Custer's exit leaves four candidates chasing an office whose main power, setting budget priorities amid a structural deficit, is currently being exercised by Springfield's inaction rather than the board itself. The most likely next move is that at least one of the remaining four, most plausibly Hilario Dominguez given the Chicago Teachers Union's stake in the outcome, will use Custer's withdrawal statement about "special interests" spending millions as a cudgel against whichever rival draws the heaviest outside money first. That fight will surface in filings with the Chicago Board of Elections within the next two weeks, since campaign finance disclosures are due on a set schedule regardless of who's still in the race.

The Austin case will move faster and cost someone their job outright. Commissioner Mike Morath has two options under Texas law, close the schools or replace the board, and the pattern from Fort Worth and Houston says he will almost certainly choose replacement, because appointing a board of managers lets the state keep the campuses open while erasing local control. The most likely timeline puts an announcement before the current school year's first grading period ends, since Morath has moved on similar triggers within weeks of the statutory threshold being met in prior districts. If Austin's elected trustees are removed, they join a list of seven districts since 2023, and the residents most affected, per the pattern in Houston and Fort Worth, will again be predominantly Black, Latino, and low-income families who had no vote in who replaces the people they elected.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

What became newly combinable this week is a template, not just a crisis — and templates travel.

A CPS Board President Race Decided Almost Entirely by Outside Money Becomes the TIF Study's Second Act. Custer's exit statement named the mechanism before anyone had to report it: millions in outside spending she wasn't willing to fight. That combination, an elected board seat with real budget authority, a structural deficit Springfield won't resolve, and four remaining candidates with institutional backers, most notably the Chicago Teachers Union behind Dominguez, is only reachable now because this is the first year Chicagoans elect a board president at all. Nobody has run this race before, which means nobody knows yet whether outside spending on a CPS board election gets tracked with anything like the rigor González's team just applied to TIF spending. The door this opens is a live test case: a University of Illinois Chicago researcher, or a Chalkbeat reporter, could apply the same public-records method, whose money moved where, and when, to board election filings due at the Chicago Board of Elections in the coming weeks. If the same opacity that let TIF run four decades without accountability shows up in campaign finance disclosures, that's not a new scandal. It's the same scandal, one election cycle later, and it means Custer's warning becomes the test case for whether Chicago can watch its own money in real time instead of waiting for a study four decades on.

That first door, a live disclosure fight inside an election Springfield's inaction has already hollowed out, is what makes the second one visible. If Dominguez or another candidate wins on a transparency platform, CPS could get a board president whose mandate is explicitly to track money the way González's team just did, structurally, not just rhetorically. That's only reachable because the study exists now, publicly, with a named methodology reporters and researchers already trust. A board president elected on the promise to apply TIF-study-style tracking to the district's own $10 billion budget, and to the state money Springfield keeps delaying, would be a genuinely new kind of oversight for CPS, not oversight imposed from outside like Morath's board of managers in Texas, but built from the same accountability instinct that produced the TIF findings in the first place. That's the door that opens toward something other than another version of the Fort Worth story. It depends entirely on whether a candidate makes that case explicitly, and on whether Illinois campaign finance law lets anyone see the money in time to matter.

Atlanta's JROTC Lawsuit Becomes a Test of Whether Federal Discrimination Law Can Do What State Takeovers Can't. O'Garro and Bonner's suit alleges a district manipulated an internal certification process to remove employees who'd already been cleared once. That combination, a federal sex-discrimination claim paired with an allegation that a school district weaponized a credentialing body, the U.S. Army Cadet Command, against its own employees, is a mechanism nobody has tested against a district's decision-making the way Morath's office tests districts against a five-year academic scorecard. If a federal judge finds Atlanta Public Schools manipulated JROTC certification the way the suit alleges, that ruling becomes a usable precedent for any staff member anywhere who suspects a district retaliated through a credentialing process rather than a personnel file, a door Texas's takeover law was never built to open or close.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The people closest to the disputed facts have the least incentive to let anyone check them.

Atlanta Public Schools Says the JROTC Instructors Are Fine Because They're Still on the Payroll. The district's statement in response to O'Garro and Bonner's lawsuit noted that "the employees referenced in the report remain employed by Atlanta Public Schools" (Fox 5 Atlanta). That's true in the narrowest sense a payroll system can register, and it's meant to land as reassurance: nobody got fired, so what's the harm. It answers a real fear, that a lawsuit means someone's income vanished, with a fact that's technically accurate and functionally beside the point. Both men were banned from district property and placed on administrative leave in July, months after contracts had already been renewed for the coming school year, and replacement instructors were hired to do the jobs they still nominally hold. "Employed" is doing the same kind of work here that "thwarted" does when officials want a story to sound cleaner than the sequence of events supports. The district benefits twice from that framing: it lets APS avoid commenting on "the specifics of active complaints or pending litigation," which is standard and defensible, while also getting a sentence into the public record that sounds like a denial without committing to one. Nobody at APS has claimed the certification packet Bonner says included a forged signature block is real, and nobody has explained why an internal tribunal cleared O'Garro in March, only for the district to sideline him four months later using the same Army credentialing process the suit says was manipulated. The gap between "still employed" and "still doing the job they were certified for" is exactly where a discrimination claim lives, and it's the gap the district's statement doesn't address because addressing it would mean litigating the case in a press release.

The district takes allegations of discrimination and retaliation seriously and follows established processes for reviewing and addressing such concerns.

Atlanta Public Schools, official statement

That sentence is true of every district being sued for the same thing right up until a judge rules otherwise, which is what makes it useful and empty at the same time. This is the same shape of gap the TIF study spent a year proving out at city scale, a public body pointing to a technically true number, employment status then, tax revenue now, while the actual mechanism of harm sits one layer down where nobody's required to look unless someone sues, or spends a year building a dataset to make them.
