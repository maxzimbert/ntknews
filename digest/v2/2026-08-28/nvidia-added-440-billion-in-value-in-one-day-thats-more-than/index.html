Nvidia Added $440 Billion in Value in One Day. That's More Than Coca-Cola, Nike and McDonald's Combined.
Nvidia and Salesforce posted blowout earnings Aug. 27, and investors bought the AI story hard.
Truths — What Happened

Nvidia's earnings call started with the stock falling and ended with $440 billion added to its market value. That reversal happened inside minutes. Nvidia shares were down when the numbers first hit, then Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress told analysts revenue could grow roughly 70 percent in the next fiscal year, well above Wall Street's expectation of about 40 percent, and the stock flipped positive and kept climbing (CNBC). By the close Thursday, Nvidia shares had gained 8.74 percent, adding roughly $440 billion in a single trading day (Techmeme). Morgan Stanley had modeled 52 percent growth for the company. Nvidia beat even that (Post and Courier).

$440 billion
The single-day gain in Nvidia's market value Aug. 27, more than the entire market capitalization of Coca-Cola.

That wasn't the only bet Wall Street had been quietly making against the AI industry, and it wasn't the only one that lost Thursday. Salesforce had spent most of the year sliding on fears that AI chatbots would let companies replace their software subscriptions entirely, a theory investors nicknamed the "SaaSpocalypse." The stock had fallen more than 40 percent from its highs, bottoming out near $150 in June (CNBC). Chief Executive Marc Benioff had been telling anyone who'd listen for two quarters that the theory was wrong. On Thursday the market finally believed him. Salesforce reported adjusted earnings of $5.90 a share against a Wall Street estimate of $3.27, and its stock jumped 22.6 percent, its second-best trading day on record (CNBC). Part of that beat came from a $2.6 billion paper gain on Salesforce's own investment in Anthropic, the AI company Benioff had once treated as an existential threat (CNBC).

This is not the SaaSpocalypse. We've been hearing about this for last two quarters, these dire predictions about the end of software and how the models eat everything, but none of them have come true for us.

Marc Benioff, CEO, Salesforce

The two companies didn't just survive the AI threat. They merged with it. Salesforce and Anthropic announced an expanded partnership called Claudeforce, letting Anthropic's Claude chatbot pull data directly from Salesforce's systems to draft emails and update customer records for corporate clients (The Wall Street Journal). The same day, Anthropic announced a separate standard letting AI systems operate physical machinery in factories and labs, a step toward AI agents that don't just answer questions but run equipment (CNBC). Not every AI story ended in relief that day. Alphabet had lost $692 billion in market value since May as investors soured on its position in the same race (Bloomberg Business). Workday's software backlog fell short of forecasts, a reminder that the rally lifted specific companies, not the whole sector (Bloomberg Business).

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The rally that began Wednesday night now has to survive a Fed chair who hasn't given his first big speech yet, and a market that's already betting on what he'll say.

Kevin Warsh will almost certainly avoid handing markets a clean signal Friday, and that ambiguity is the point. Economists watching his first Jackson Hole address as Fed chair expect him to lean into skepticism about "previous policy assumptions" without committing to a rate path, an approach one Natixis economist described as "anti-doctrinaire" rather than declarative (Post and Courier). That vagueness is a bet in itself. Wednesday's inflation reading came in hotter than expected, which means Warsh has cover to sound cautious even while chip and software stocks are behaving like the economy has no ceiling. If he leans hawkish, the same investors who spent Thursday afternoon buying Nvidia and Salesforce on credit will be the first ones selling Monday morning, and the sectors that already sat out the rally, everything outside tech, will absorb the next leg down.

That split market is the more durable problem, and it will keep showing up stock by stock over the next two weeks. Nvidia's own earnings call should have lifted the entire AI infrastructure trade, but hyperscaler-adjacent chip stocks broadly underperformed even as software names went vertical, a divergence CNBC's Investing Club desk flagged the same afternoon without a clean explanation (CNBC). The most likely explanation, and the one that will keep recurring through September earnings season, is money rotating rather than money arriving: funds covering short positions in software are selling AI infrastructure names to do it. That means the next earnings report, whether it's Marvell, Snowflake, or the crowded slate of software names reporting this month, gets read less as a verdict on that company and more as a referendum on which side of the trade currently has the capital. Snowflake already has a raised price target waiting on it before it even reports (CNBC).

Anthropic is the actor best positioned to win regardless of which way that rotation goes, and its next 14 days should make that plainer. It's now embedded inside Salesforce's earnings story through Claudeforce, inside CrowdStrike's pitch to cybersecurity buyers, and pushing into factory floors through its new hardware standard, three separate revenue paths that don't depend on each other holding up simultaneously (CNBC). A company that valuable, heading toward a public offering, has every incentive to keep announcing partnerships on exactly this kind of week. Expect at least one more before Anthropic's IPO roadshow begins in earnest.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

If Anthropic's hardware standard actually gets adopted, the company stops being a chatbot vendor with a Salesforce deal and starts being infrastructure — and infrastructure changes who owns the AI story next.

Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard could turn factory floors into the next earnings narrative, the way Claudeforce just did for enterprise software. The standard is still in research preview, limited to a handful of science, robotics, and manufacturing partners, but Anthropic has already said it plans to open-source it, the same path it took with the Model Context Protocol in 2024 (CNBC). Once a standard is open, any device maker can build to it without paying Anthropic for the privilege, which means the leverage isn't in the software license, it's in being first. If MHS gets adopted the way MCP did, Anthropic becomes the company whose interface every industrial robot and lab instrument speaks, not because it manufactures anything, but because it wrote the language first. That's a door that wasn't reachable a year ago, when Anthropic was still mostly known for a chatbot competing with OpenAI's. Now it has Salesforce's balance sheet, a manufacturing standard, and an IPO roadshow all lining up in the same six months.

Walk through that door and a second one opens: Amazon's 2 million-GPU order for Nvidia hardware stops being just a chip story and becomes a bet on who runs the software layer on top of it. Amazon Web Services is buying Nvidia's GPUs and Vera CPUs at a scale that assumes years of AI compute demand, but compute alone doesn't decide which company's agents actually operate the equipment those chips power (CNBC). If Anthropic's hardware standard becomes the default way AI agents talk to physical machines, Amazon's hyperscale infrastructure and Anthropic's agent layer become complementary rather than competitive, the same way Salesforce and Anthropic just proved a supposed rival could become a revenue partner instead. That's not a guaranteed alliance. It's a combination that wasn't sitting on the table before Thursday, when Anthropic was a model provider and Amazon was a chip buyer with its own competing chip ambitions. Now there's a shared incentive neither company had a reason to act on last month.

There's a version of this that doesn't just concentrate power, and it's worth naming. If MHS actually goes open source the way Anthropic says, smaller manufacturers, the ones without the capital to build proprietary AI integration the way Amazon or Google can, get access to the same standard for free. A mid-sized robotics shop or a university lab, the kind of institution priced out of custom AI infrastructure, could plug into MHS the same week a Fortune 500 company does. That's the generative door: a standard built by one of the most valuable private companies in the world, made available to whoever wants it, at the exact moment memory shortages and capital costs are making it harder for smaller players to compete in AI at all. Whether Anthropic actually opens the gate on schedule, or slow-walks it the way plenty of "eventually open-source" promises have slow-walked before, is the thing to watch once the research preview ends.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The word Wall Street reached for this week wasn't "profit." It was "vindication" — and vindication is a story someone gets to tell only if the other side stays quiet.

Jim Cramer called it "lies and damn lies" that Nvidia and Salesforce had just defeated, and the framing does more work than the earnings did. On air Thursday, Cramer said the market had been "in the grip of some lies and damn lies, backed by chimerical statistics," and that both bear cases "backfired" (CNBC). It's a satisfying story if you own the stocks, and Cramer's charitable trust owns Salesforce. The claim being buried inside that framing is that the doubts about AI profitability were dishonest in the first place, rather than reasonable questions that one strong quarter doesn't fully answer.

Here's why the "lie" framing is doing more than describing a good earnings beat: it forecloses the next question before anyone gets to ask it. A skeptic who points out that Nvidia has flagged memory shortages as a real constraint on growth, or that Salesforce's blowout quarter included a $2.6 billion paper gain from its own stake in Anthropic rather than pure operating strength, isn't correcting the record. In Cramer's frame, they're the next liar in line, peddling another "canard" that will eventually "backfire" too. That's a useful trick for anyone with a position to defend, because it turns every future doubt into evidence of the doubter's bad faith rather than a fair reading of the numbers.

The mechanism benefits more than one party. It benefits Cramer's credibility as a host who called the rally before it happened, told viewers Thursday that "if you listened to me and just owned these stocks... you had a phenomenal day." It benefits Marc Benioff, who gets to retroactively cast two quarters of investor skepticism as a "nonsense" narrative rather than a legitimate market response to AI's disruption risk, the same risk Salesforce itself is racing to defuse by partnering with Anthropic instead of competing with it. And it benefits every fund that got caught short on these stocks and needed a story bigger than "we guessed wrong" to explain a bad week; "the bears were lying" reads better in a client letter than "the bears had a point and we ignored the upside case."

We were in the grip of some lies and damn lies, backed by chimerical statistics, and now both bear stories backfired.

Jim Cramer, CNBC

None of this means the SaaSpocalypse thesis was correct. It wasn't, not this quarter. But a bear case that turns out wrong in one earnings cycle isn't the same as a bear case that was fraudulent all along, and Wall Street has a long habit of calling the losing side of a trade liars right up until the cycle turns and the labels flip back. Alphabet's investors, still down $692 billion since May on the same underlying AI questions, are about to find out which version of that story applies to them.
