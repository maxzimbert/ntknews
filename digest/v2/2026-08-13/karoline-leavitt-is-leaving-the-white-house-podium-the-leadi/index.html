Karoline Leavitt Is Leaving the White House Podium. The Leading Replacement Is Still on CNN's Payroll.

Karoline Leavitt is exiting as White House press secretary, and the scramble to replace her has surfaced a CNN pundit as a frontrunner.

Truths

The Press Secretary Job Is Open, and the Candidate Pool Is Telling

Karoline Leavitt is leaving the White House press secretary role within weeks, and the names circulating as replacements reveal as much about the administration's priorities as any policy announcement would. According to reporting from multiple outlets, CNN contributor Scott Jennings — a Republican commentator who has made a second career defending Trump positions on air to skeptical anchors — has emerged as a frontrunner (Fox News). Also in the mix: Natalie Harp, a 35-year-old Trump aide whose profile surfaced alongside coverage that led with her hair color before her credentials (Daily Beast).

Jennings hasn't left CNN. He's already on record responding publicly to the speculation — one headline called it an audition, and the framing was not flattering (Atlanta Black Star). A spokesperson who signals availability through cable news appearances before the job is offered is advertising something, and it isn't discretion.

The Pattern Here Is the Point

That's not just a detail about Jennings. It's the shape of how this White House has consistently approached the role — as a performance slot first, a communications function second. Leavitt came up through Trump's media orbit before taking the podium. The current shortlist pulls from the same ecosystem: people already practiced at playing to a camera, already fluent in the register the administration wants amplified. What the job requires and what it rewards have been the same thing for long enough that no one in the building seems to notice the distinction anymore.

3 named candidates, 0 with traditional government communications backgrounds
Every frontrunner surfaced so far has a television credit before a policy one.

The press secretary's job is, formally, to represent the public's interest in knowing what the executive branch is doing. In practice, it has become something closer to the opposite — a buffer between the press and the people making decisions, staffed by someone optimized for the buffer, not the transparency. That's not new to this administration, but the open audition process makes the mechanism unusually visible.

Probabilities

Whoever Gets the Podium Will Be Optimized for the Same Job Leavitt Had

Scott Jennings will almost certainly leave CNN within the next two weeks, and the departure will be framed as public service.

The audition-in-plain-sight posture Truths described has a predictable next move: the network separation that makes the hire look principled rather than pre-negotiated. CNN will accept the resignation gracefully — Jennings has been useful to them, but a sitting White House press secretary on their payroll is a different kind of liability than a commentator who defends Trump at the roundtable. The White House gets to announce a "respected voice from across the aisle." Jennings gets the title. CNN gets the clip packages. The mechanism that produces these outcomes is not complicated, and no one in it has an incentive to slow it down.

What that means for the press corps is the more durable question. A spokesperson who spent years modeling how to hold a MAGA position while seated next to people who disagree with it has developed a specific skill — not explaining policy, but absorbing friction without conceding ground. That's precisely what the role has been rewarding. The briefing room doesn't get a more forthcoming press secretary; it gets one who is better trained at the performance of engagement.

If Jennings doesn't take it, the job will almost certainly go to someone with an identical profile and a shorter paper trail.

Natalie Harp is the clearest backup position, and the coverage that surfaced her — leading with appearance before credentials — was itself a form of vetting, the administration's ecosystem testing whether she lands. If Jennings creates complications, either through his CNN contract or a last-minute repositioning, Harp steps into a slot that has already been shaped by the same logic: visible loyalty, camera fluency, minimal footprint outside the Trump orbit. The risk profile is lower. So is the name recognition, which in this role isn't necessarily a disadvantage — a press secretary nobody outside MAGA circles knows well is harder to make into a liability.

The structural bet the administration is making, whoever takes the podium, is that the press secretary's job remains a performance problem rather than a credibility one. That bet has paid off consistently enough that there's no pressure to revise it. The person who loses in either scenario is the same: the reporter at the briefing room who came with a question that needed a straight answer.

Possibilities

The part of this story almost nobody is asking about is what happens to the briefing room if the audition model becomes the template — not just for this vacancy, but for the press secretary role permanently.

If Jennings takes the job and performs well by the administration's definition, the next White House — of either party — will have a new benchmark for what the position is.

The conditions for this are already in place. A CNN commentator stepping directly from cable roundtable to White House podium doesn't just blur the line between media and government — it dissolves the pretense that the line was ever the point. What comes after is a precedent problem. Future administrations looking for a press secretary will have a proof of concept: televised audition, ideological legibility, camera discipline. The journalism schools that still teach government communications as a distinct craft will be training for a job that no longer quite exists. The reporters asking questions at the podium will be facing someone who spent years not answering them — not evasively, but structurally, because the format never required it. That's a different kind of unanswerable than stonewalling. It's fluent non-disclosure, and it's harder to call out live on air.

The hopeful version of this — and it's real, not consolation — is that the visibility of the audition process has already triggered exactly the kind of coverage that makes the mechanism harder to run quietly next time. The headlines describing Jennings as auditioning, the framing of Harp's coverage as a tell about how the administration values the role — that's the press doing something the press secretary's job is supposed to prevent: naming the machinery while it runs (Atlanta Black Star). If that coverage accumulates into a durable shorthand — "the Jennings model," the way "revolving door" became a phrase that actually changed some behavior — the audition-in-plain-sight approach becomes costlier to run in the open. The next administration doesn't have to be a different party for the pressure to land; it just has to be one where someone in the room finds the optics inconvenient.

The low-probability scenario worth watching is a Jennings confirmation that fractures CNN's remaining credibility with the center-right audience it has spent three years courting.

CNN brought Jennings in specifically to hold viewers who felt the network had drifted left. If he walks into a White House role after running what one outlet already called an audition, the implicit contract with that audience — *we give you a Republican who says what he actually thinks* — gets exposed as a casting decision rather than a journalistic one. The viewers who trusted the performance have reason to feel used. That's a small audience in absolute terms, but it's the audience CNN least has alternatives for (Daily Beast). Networks have shed credibility over smaller reveals than this. The question isn't whether they can survive it — they can — but whether the Jennings slot gets filled by someone who learned from watching how it ended.

Lies

The Lie the Audition Was Designed to Tell

Scott Jennings Is "Considering" the Job. He Was Never Not Taking It.

The claim circulating — amplified by Jennings himself through his public response to the speculation — is that this is an open process, a genuine search, a man weighing his options. The framing invites the audience to see deliberation where the Truths section already showed choreography. Jennings hasn't pushed back. He's performed consideration, which is a different thing, and the performance has been running long enough that the audition was the answer.

Who believes it, and why: the center-right CNN viewer who decided Jennings was the honest Republican in the room — the one who told hard truths to MAGA audiences while keeping his integrity intact. For that viewer, a man of genuine principle would actually pause before taking a partisan government job. The pause feels like evidence. It isn't. It's stagecraft for an audience that needs to feel the choice was real.

Who benefits from the belief spreading: Jennings, who arrives at the podium looking recruited rather than angling; the White House, which gets to call it a bipartisan get rather than a loyalty hire; and CNN, which avoids the sharper version of the story — that it spent three years paying someone to rehearse for a job he intended to take. The "considering" frame distributes the embarrassment so evenly that nobody has to absorb it.

He's already auditioning

Atlanta Black Star, headline characterization of Jennings' public response to the speculation

The Atlanta Black Star headline landed this with more precision than most straight reporting managed. An audition announced in public — through public statements, through the performance of openness — is not a job search. It's the last scene of a negotiation that concluded earlier, played out for optics. The tell is that Jennings didn't decline the framing. A person genuinely undecided about leaving a major network platform doesn't respond in ways one outlet describes as "painfully on brand" (Atlanta Black Star). A person running out the clock on a decision already made does.

The lie isn't that Jennings wants the job. That's just ambition, and there's nothing wrong with it. The lie is the theater of uncertainty — the "I'm flattered, I'm thinking" posture that lets every party to the arrangement keep its story clean a little longer. The press secretary's official job is to be the administration's honest voice to the public. The audition for that job opened with a performance that was the opposite. That's not an inauspicious start. That's the whole résumé.
