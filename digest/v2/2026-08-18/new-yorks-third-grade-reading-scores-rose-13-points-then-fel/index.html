New York's Third-Grade Reading Scores Rose 13 Points, Then Fell 14. Nobody Can Explain the Whiplash.
NYC reading scores swung wildly for two years running. Testing experts want an audit before anyone claims progress or blame.
Truths — What Happened

Half of New York City students tested proficient in reading last year, down 6 percentage points from the year before. That number alone might read as a bad year. It doesn't explain why third grade scores fell 14 points after climbing 13 the year before, or why fourth and fifth grade saw similar swings while seventh and eighth grade barely moved (Chalkbeat). Math scores rose statewide and stayed flat in the city, which is its own quiet detail. Reading is the subject that got a curriculum overhaul and a computer-based testing switch in the same stretch. Math got neither.

That timing is why Marianne Perie, who co-chairs the state's technical advisory committee and consults with states on testing for a living, isn't ready to call last year's jump real. "My instinctive reaction is there may have been an issue last year that we missed," she said, adding that a big increase followed by a big decrease is the kind of pattern that makes psychometricians nervous, not confident (Chalkbeat). Her committee meets next month to look for an explanation. EdTrust-New York didn't wait. The nonprofit called for an independent audit last week, arguing officials never explained last year's surge in the first place, which is exactly what makes this year's crash impossible to read (Chalkbeat).

The people whose credibility is on the line responded on cue. NWEA, the company that builds and sells the state's test, said its own due diligence found no anomalies and that it has "high confidence in the accuracy of the results," according to spokesperson Simona Beattie (Chalkbeat). Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels called for "rigorous analysis" without conceding the scores were wrong. The teachers union took the opposite lesson, pointing to the decline as proof the city's mandated curriculum has failed and threatening to pull its support.

This kind of big increase followed by a big decrease is surprising.

Marianne Perie, co-chair, New York state technical advisory committee

None of those positions actually accounts for the pattern. The union's theory can't explain why reading scores also swung in districts that never touched the curriculum, a gap Chalkbeat's reporting flags directly. Benjamin Shear, a testing expert at the University of Colorado Boulder, put it plainly: the fluctuations were unusual enough that they require scrutiny before anyone, city officials or the union, gets to claim vindication (Chalkbeat). The state education department says it will investigate. Whether that investigation lands before next year's scores get spun the same way, by whoever needs them to, is the part nobody's promised yet.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The technical advisory committee meets next month. Whoever's story survives that meeting gets to write next year's headline.

The Audit Question Gets Answered Before the Committee Even Meets. EdTrust-New York's call for an independent audit puts pressure on a state education department that has a habit of promising analysis and delivering timelines instead (Chalkbeat). The most likely outcome in the next two weeks is not an audit. It's a statement from JP O'Hare's office reaffirming that a "detailed analysis" is underway, without a named auditor, a deadline, or independence from NWEA, the vendor that already declared itself confident in its own results. That distinction matters because NWEA grading its own test is not the same as EdTrust's ask, and the state knows the difference well enough to blur it rather than answer it. Watch for whether Perie's committee is given authority to review NWEA's raw item-level data or just the summary statistics NWEA has already released. If it's the latter, the audit question doesn't get resolved. It gets postponed into next year's testing cycle, which is exactly the outcome that protects everyone who doesn't want this year's number to be the real one.

That postponement sets up the next collision.

Chancellor Samuels Will Have to Pick a Side He's Currently Straddling. Samuels called for "rigorous analysis" without saying whether he believes the scores are accurate, a position that works for exactly as long as nobody forces a decision. The teachers union has already made its bet, using the decline to argue the mandated curriculum failed, and it will almost certainly escalate that argument publicly if Samuels doesn't respond within the next month. The mechanism here isn't pedagogical, it's political. If Samuels defends the curriculum before the technical committee reports back, he's betting his credibility on a number Perie herself won't yet call real. If he distances himself from it, he hands the union the win it's already claiming and undercuts the rationale for last year's victory lap. The most likely move is a delay tactic, some version of "let's wait for the committee," which keeps Samuels out of the blast radius but leaves principals and reading teachers in city schools making instructional decisions this fall with no clarity on which of the last two years was the fluke.

The state's technical advisory committee was built for exactly this kind of dispute. Whether it's given the data to actually settle it, or just enough room to look like it tried, is what the next month will show.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The committee's authority is still undefined, and that gap is what makes the next year's testing cycle a genuinely open door.

Perie's Committee Asks for Item-Level Data and NWEA Says No. The Probabilities section already flagged the fork: summary statistics versus raw item-level data. What's newly combinable is the specific standing of the technical advisory committee itself. It's a state-paid panel with a mandate to investigate, meeting on a fixed date, chaired by someone who's already gone on record doubting the numbers before the vendor has to answer to her directly. NWEA has never had to say no to that combination before, because the pattern strong enough to trigger this kind of scrutiny has never happened two years running. If Perie's committee formally requests raw data and NWEA declines, citing proprietary test security, the fight stops being about test scores and becomes a fight about who owns the evidence needed to grade a public school system. That's a door nobody walked through last week because the request hadn't been made yet. Once it is, EdTrust-New York has a new and sharper ask: not an audit of scores, but a public accounting of what a state-funded testing vendor is allowed to withhold from the state's own oversight panel.

That standoff, if it happens, opens a second door that only exists because of the first.

A Refusal Becomes the Case for Public Ownership of the Test. If NWEA says no to the committee, the argument shifts from this test cycle in Chalkbeat's account to New York's decision to depend on a single private vendor for the instrument that decides whether a curriculum overhaul gets called a success or a failure. That's not speculative escalation. It's the same tension already sitting in the record: the teachers union blaming curriculum, the chancellor withholding judgment, EdTrust asking for independence NWEA hasn't granted. A refusal on data access would hand every one of those actors a shared target for the first time. Unions rarely align with reform-minded nonprofits like EdTrust, but a vendor stonewalling a state's own technical panel is the kind of grievance that doesn't require them to agree on the curriculum question at all. That alignment is the generative door. It's the version of this story where the fight over one bad test cycle produces a push for a testing system New York actually controls, audited by people who don't work for the company being graded.

A Cleaner Root Cause Gets Named Before the Union or the Chancellor Commit Further. The one variable Truths already surfaced and nobody has explained is the platform switch itself. Reading moved to computer-based testing in the same window the curriculum changed. Math didn't move platforms and didn't swing. If the committee's first finding, ahead of any fight over NWEA's raw data, is that the format change itself produced the distortion, that's a door that closes the union's curriculum argument and the chancellor's curriculum defense at the same time, because neither side's theory survives a testing-format explanation. Reachable only because both combatants have already staked public positions that a mechanical answer would embarrass equally.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Two theories are already circulating about why the scores swung, and neither one survives contact with the data everyone's already read.

The Curriculum Overhaul Is Either the Whole Story or a Complete Fiction, Depending on Who's Talking. City officials who took credit for last year's surge and the teachers union that now blames this year's crash on the exact same curriculum mandate are both making a claim the underlying data doesn't support. Officials need the curriculum to have worked because they built a political case on it. The union needs it to have failed because it's already threatened to withdraw support and needs a reason that isn't "we don't actually know yet." Both stories offer something real: certainty, in a situation where the state's own technical advisory committee hasn't met yet and the chancellor himself has only called for "rigorous analysis" without saying which year he believes. But Chalkbeat's reporting already contains the fact that breaks both claims. Districts that never touched the curriculum saw similar swings, and math scores, tested on the same students in the same schools, didn't move the same way. A curriculum can't explain a pattern that shows up where the curriculum was never installed. What benefits from the story anyway is obvious. If officials get to keep the win, the mandate survives budget season intact. If the union gets to call it a failure, the mandate becomes the thing they finally defeated. Neither side has an incentive to wait for Perie's committee, because the committee's actual answer, whether it's a testing-platform artifact nobody planned for, is a story where nobody gets to be right.

NWEA's "High Confidence" Is a Claim About Its Own Homework, Not an Independent Finding. Simona Beattie's statement that NWEA found no anomalies and has "high confidence in the accuracy of the results" is being read by some as the closest thing to a clean bill of health this story has gotten. It answers a real fear, that the whole testing apparatus might be broken and nobody would know until a generation of third graders had already been sorted by it. But the confidence is self-graded. NWEA is the vendor that built the test, administers the test, and profits from New York continuing to use the test, checking its own work and pronouncing it sound before the state's own technical advisory committee has even convened. That's not evidence the results are right. It's evidence NWEA has more to lose from being wrong than most parties in this story, which is exactly why EdTrust-New York asked for an audit that isn't NWEA grading NWEA. Should Perie's committee request the raw item-level data behind that confidence and get a refusal, the claim collapses into what it always was: a company's assurance about its own product, offered before anyone with the authority to check has been given the chance.
