Meta Told Jurors "The Young Ones Are the Best Ones." A Federal Trial Now Asks If That Sentence Is Worth $1.4 Trillion.
Trial opened in Oakland federal court. Twenty-nine states accuse Meta of hiding teen addiction research.
Truths — What Happened

Twenty-nine states walked into U.S. District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers's Oakland courtroom with Meta's internal paperwork as their star witness. California deputy attorney general Megan O'Neill opened by reading from company documents she said proved Meta knew exactly what it was doing: one internal note calling young users "the best ones," another stating plainly that "teens are hooked despite how it makes them feel" (Irish Times).

They knew. Time and again, profits won.

Megan O'Neill, deputy attorney general for California

Meta's attorney Paul Schmidt didn't dispute that people struggle with social media use, but he pushed back hard on the word "addiction" itself, telling jurors the company had already built tools to address the struggle (LBC). That's a narrower argument than it sounds. Denying the word "addiction" while conceding "struggle" is not really a denial — it's a rebranding, and the jury will decide if the difference matters.

That distinction mattered less once Arturo Béjar took the stand. Béjar, a former senior engineering and product leader at Facebook who returned to the company in 2019 after his own 14-year-old daughter started fielding unsolicited requests for nude photos, testified that Instagram had become "a product that uses you" and that Meta's internal research on harm to young users was never acted on (LBC). He'd made this case before Congress already; this time he was making it to twelve jurors with the power to attach a number to it.

The number on the table is large enough to change how the company operates, not just how it apologizes. The states are seeking remedies they want applied nationwide, not just in California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey — the four states formally party to the case — and Meta is facing exposure reported as high as $1.4 trillion (Daily Mail). Meta says it "strongly disagrees" with the child-privacy claims and points to what it calls a longstanding commitment to young users (Guardian).

This isn't Meta's first verdict on this exact question. A Los Angeles jury already found Instagram and YouTube deliberately engineered to be addictive, awarding $6 million in a related case brought by a plaintiff identified only by initials (Wikipedia). Oakland is that case scaled up by every zero the word "nationwide" can add.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The jury that decides Meta's fate is now watching the company's own lawyers pick a fight with the word "addiction" — and that fight is likely to define the next two weeks.

Meta's Defense Team Will Keep Litigating the Dictionary, Not the Documents. Paul Schmidt already conceded that people "struggle" with Instagram while refusing to call it addiction, and the most likely next move is a parade of expert witnesses paid to draw a clinical line between compulsive use and diagnosable addiction. That's a defense built for a jury instruction, not a jury's gut. Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers will almost certainly be asked in the next week to rule on how the term gets defined for jurors, and whichever definition she picks will shape closing arguments more than any single document O'Neill reads aloud. Meta's lawyers know this, which is why they're fighting over vocabulary in week one instead of waiting for week four.

That semantic battle only matters if the jury believes Meta knew what its own research showed, which is where Arturo Béjar's testimony keeps doing work even after he's left the stand. The states will almost certainly call additional former Meta employees or outside researchers in the next 10 to 14 days to corroborate his claim that internal warnings were shelved, because a single whistleblower is a story and two are a pattern. Watch for Meta to respond by trying to narrow each new witness to their specific role and timeframe, arguing that no one person saw the whole picture. That's a technically true defense that gets harder to sell with every new name attached to it.

The bigger mechanism sits above both fights. This is the second time in 2026 a jury has been asked whether Meta engineered addiction on purpose, and the Los Angeles verdict already answered yes at a scale of millions. Oakland's outcome will almost certainly shape settlement leverage in every other pending state case against Meta, whether or not this trial reaches a verdict before a deal gets cut. A company facing potential exposure in the trillions has every incentive to settle quietly the moment the evidence stops going its way, and Meta's stock price gives it a 14-day clock that has nothing to do with the judge's calendar. If Meta's legal team senses the jury is leaning toward "they knew," the most likely outcome isn't a verdict at all. It's a settlement announced on a Friday afternoon, timed to be old news by Monday.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

A settlement announced on a Friday isn't the end of this story — it's the door that opens the next room, and what's sitting in that room is every other lawsuit Meta hasn't settled yet.

A Settlement in Oakland Becomes the Opening Bid in Every Other Statehouse. If Meta cuts a deal before this jury reaches a verdict, the number attached to it becomes the floor, not the ceiling, for every attorney general watching from outside California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey. Twenty-five states signed onto the broader 2023 filing without becoming formal parties to this specific trial, which means twenty-five sets of lawyers are free to use whatever Meta agrees to pay here as their opening number in negotiations that haven't started yet. That's newly reachable specifically because of what the K.G.M. verdict already did and what this trial is doing now — two juries in two years finding the same design choices deliberate turns a settlement from a one-time cost into a template other states can cite by name. Meta's lawyers know this too, which is part of why a quiet settlement is more likely than a public one. A number that never gets read aloud in open court is a number that's harder for the next state's attorney general to point to.

That template only becomes a real threat to Meta's business model if it reaches beyond the states and into Congress, which is the second door this trial has left cracked open. Arturo Béjar already testified about the same failures to a Senate subcommittee before he ever took the Oakland stand, and a jury verdict that agrees with him gives that testimony something it didn't have in front of senators — a finding of fact, reached by twelve people with no reelection to worry about. A verdict or settlement that concedes deliberate design toward addiction is the kind of fact pattern that turns stalled child-safety legislation from a partisan stalemate into a bill with a jury's fingerprints on it. That's not guaranteed. Congress has watched worse findings die in committee before. But it wasn't reachable last month, before this jury heard the words "the young ones are the best ones" read into the record, and it is reachable now.

There's a version of this that doesn't end with Meta simply absorbing a cost and moving on, and it's worth naming because the doors this section opens don't all lead somewhere worse. A settlement large enough and public enough could force Meta into court-monitored design changes, the same lever that reshaped how tobacco companies were allowed to advertise after their own documents became evidence. Nothing in this trial guarantees that outcome. But the combination is sitting in the room for the first time. Two juries, twenty-nine states and a whistleblower with a Senate transcript are not, on their own, new. A company facing that combination all at once, with its own words already in evidence, is.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Meta's lawyers aren't just defending the company in court — they're running a second argument outside it, and that one doesn't have a judge policing it for accuracy.

"There Is No Such Thing as Social Media Addiction," Meta's Defense Implies. The American Psychiatric Association Disagrees With the Framing, Even If It Hasn't Settled the Clinical Term. Paul Schmidt's opening didn't deny that people struggle with Instagram. It denied the word attached to that struggle, and that distinction is doing more work outside the courtroom than inside it. Meta's public messaging in the days around the trial has leaned on a version of this argument stripped of Schmidt's careful hedging: that "addiction" is activist language, not science, and that a company can't be held liable for a diagnosis that doesn't formally exist in the DSM the way substance addiction does. It's a claim built for people who want permission to stop worrying about their kid's phone, because "there's no medical consensus" sounds a lot cleaner than "we don't know yet and neither does the company that built it." The people who benefit from that permission slip aren't the parents. They're Meta's shareholders, who need the jury and the public to hear "unproven" instead of "unaddressed."

The mechanism is a bait-and-switch on the burden of proof. Nobody in this trial has to prove social media addiction meets a clinical diagnostic threshold to win. The states have to prove Meta's own researchers described compulsive, harmful use internally, then buried it, which is a different and much narrower claim than "addiction is real, full stop." Meta knows this. Schmidt's actual legal argument, conceding "struggle" while contesting "addiction," is a lawyer's argument, precise and survivable. The company's broader public posture, letting "no such thing as addiction" circulate as the takeaway headline, is a marketing argument, and it's the one more likely to reach a parent scrolling past a news alert who never reads the transcript.

The tell is the gap between what Schmidt said in the room and what Meta wants repeated outside it. A company confident the science was on its side wouldn't need its own internal documents to say "teens are hooked despite how it makes them feel." It would just say the documents don't exist, or don't mean what the states claim. Meta hasn't tried that. It's arguing about the label on a box it already admitted was full.
