Amazon Cut the Spines Off Rare Books to Feed Its AI. A Tracking Device Followed One Straight to Las Vegas.
A 404 Media investigation traced a rare book shipment to an Amazon AI training facility.
Truths — What Happened

Rare book dealers started noticing something strange about a year ago. Orders were arriving faster than usual, in batches that made no sense — a history of the Ottoman Empire next to a manual on Midwestern crop rotation, connected by nothing except an ISBN and a buyer who didn't seem to care what anything cost (Talking Points Memo). Reporter Emanuel Maiberg decided to find out who was buying. He and 404 Media planted a tracking device inside a rare book, fed it into the same clearinghouse system the peddlers use, and watched where it went (404 Media).

It went to Las Vegas, to an Amazon facility called VGT3 — its internal signage a dinosaur clutching a book in its claws, an image someone at Amazon apparently thought was cute enough to put on a wall (TechCrunch). Inside, workers cut the spines off books, run the loose pages through industrial scanners, and throw the remains away. Amazon confirmed to 404 Media that it buys books through commercial channels to improve its products, which is the kind of sentence a company writes when it does not want to say the word "shredded."

That confirms what the used-book trade had already worked out on its own. The reason a company that sells nearly everything on earth needs a warehouse for spine removal comes down to a math problem AI researchers have spent two years trying to avoid. Large language models trained on the open internet have already eaten most of what's there, and a growing share of what's left was written by other AI models — a diet that degrades the next model's output the way a photocopy of a photocopy degrades, a failure mode researchers call model collapse (TechCrunch). A book printed before 2022 comes with a guarantee no scraped webpage can offer anymore: a human wrote every word of it.

That scarcity is already reordering the industry the books came from. Publishing deals are collapsing under suspicion that a manuscript was AI-assisted, agents pulling out of contracts they'd signed months earlier once doubts surfaced about a book's authorship (The Wall Street Journal). The people who once made a living selling old books to readers are now selling them, unknowingly, to a machine that will never read them at all.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The rare-book trade is small enough that one buyer with no price limit can move the entire market — and Amazon just proved it's that buyer.

Book Peddlers Will Keep Selling to the Machine, Because They Don't Know They're Doing It The clearinghouse system that let Amazon buy anonymously will almost certainly stay in place for months, not because sellers are naive but because the system was built to protect buyers from sellers marking up prices once they know who's asking. Dealers who suspect they're feeding an AI training pipeline have no easy way to confirm it or refuse the sale without losing the order to a competitor. Expect 404 Media or a similar outlet to run a second tracking experiment within the next two weeks, this time aimed at a different AI company, now that the method has been proven and the story has attention. The dealer who talks on the record first, breaking the trade's habit of quiet complicity, is the one who ends up shaping how this gets regulated.

That silence won't hold everywhere. The publishing houses are the next pressure point, and the mechanism is already visible in the deals falling apart over suspected AI authorship. The most likely outcome in the next two weeks is at least one more agent or publisher publicly canceling a contract, and doing it faster and louder than the cases already reported, because no agency wants to be the one still defending a manuscript when the doubts turn out to be true. Authors without a paper trail proving they wrote a book alone — drafts, revision history, correspondence with an editor — are the ones positioned to lose those fights, whether or not they used AI at all, because suspicion alone is now enough to void a deal.

The harder question is what Amazon does once dealers start pricing in the risk.

Amazon purchases books through commercial channels to improve the products and services customers use.

Amazon, statement to 404 Media

A statement that thin is built to survive one news cycle, not a congressional letter. The most likely next step is that a member of Congress, probably one already positioned on antitrust or AI oversight, sends Amazon a formal inquiry within the next two weeks asking how many books it has purchased and destroyed and whether any were the only surviving copies. Amazon will almost certainly decline to give a number. Whether that number ever becomes public depends less on Amazon than on whether the rare-book trade decides it has more to gain from talking than from staying inside the clearinghouse's comfortable anonymity.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

If dealers start pricing in the risk, the anonymity that made this whole operation possible starts to break down — and that opens rooms nobody's mapped yet.

A Provenance Market Could Grow Up Around the Thing That Made Books Valuable in the First Place The clearinghouse system worked because sellers didn't know who was buying. Once enough dealers suspect — or confirm, through their own tracking devices — that Amazon or another AI company is on the other end, the next combinable move is obvious: dealers start selling documented human authorship as a separate, priced feature, the way diamond sellers sell conflict-free certification. A first edition with a verified chain of ownership back to a human author becomes worth more than an identical book without one, not for collectors but for AI companies desperate to prove their training data is clean. Anthropic already has a pirated-books lawsuit hanging over it, and that legal exposure is the incentive that didn't exist a year ago and does now. A dealer who starts offering sworn provenance statements alongside a sale isn't protecting the book anymore. They're pricing the one thing a scraped webpage can never fake.

That market only exists if the first door opens: sellers willing to break from clearinghouse anonymity and deal directly with AI companies who want to buy clean. If it does, the second-order possibility is a licensing structure that looks less like a used-book sale and more like a stock-photo contract, and it's only visible from inside that first room. Publishers sitting on backlists of pre-2022 titles, the same houses now watching new manuscripts collapse under AI suspicion, control an asset they haven't priced yet: decades of verifiably human text they already own the rights to. Random House or Penguin licensing an out-of-print backlist directly to an AI company, at a negotiated rate instead of letting the books get destroyed at $40 a copy through a clearinghouse, is a deal that wasn't reachable last month because no publisher had public proof that the alternative was a shredder in Las Vegas. Now there's a photograph of a dinosaur eating a book to point to in the negotiation.

The generative version of that door is the one worth watching. A publisher that licenses its backlist instead of losing it to anonymous buyers keeps the books intact, gets paid for training data it already owns, and gives its authors' estates a royalty stream nobody previously accounted for. It also gives Amazon, or whichever AI company moves first, a clean provenance story to hand the next congressional inquiry instead of a one-line statement about commercial channels. None of that existed as an option before this week, because before this week nobody outside 404 Media could prove where the books were actually going. Now everyone in that supply chain — dealer, publisher, author's estate, AI lab — knows exactly what's at stake in refusing to negotiate, which is the only thing that reliably moves an industry this slow.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Amazon's statement to 404 Media wasn't a lie so much as a container built to hold one — and the container is doing exactly what it was built for.

"We Purchase Books Through Commercial Channels to Improve the Products and Services Customers Use" That's the entirety of Amazon's public explanation for why it's buying rare books, cutting off the spines, scanning the pages and throwing away what's left (404 Media). The sentence is true in the way a guilty plea to jaywalking is true when the charge on the table is grand theft. It answers a question nobody asked — does Amazon buy books commercially, yes, obviously — while declining every question that matters. How many books. Whether any were the last known copy of anything. Whether "improve the products and services" means Alexa's autocomplete or the frontier model Amazon is racing Anthropic and OpenAI to train. A company happy to answer would have answered. Amazon's own name for the facility, a dinosaur cradling a book in its claws, suggests somebody inside the building understood the joke and decided to let it stand rather than explain it (TechCrunch).

The people most likely to believe the statement, or at least not push on it, are exactly the people it's aimed at. A reader skimming a headline sees a familiar company doing a familiar thing, buying inventory to run a business, and moves on. That's the fear the statement is built to soothe. Nobody wants to think the company that delivers their paper towels is also feeding a shredder in Las Vegas with books that may exist nowhere else on earth. The statement gives that reader permission to stop looking, which is the same permission every vague corporate line has offered since companies first learned that a sentence with no numbers in it can't be fact-checked into a scandal.

Who benefits is the more useful question, because it isn't really Amazon's PR team. It's Amazon's legal team, buying time before the number becomes public, and it's Amazon's AI division, buying cover while a book that took someone a lifetime to write gets fed to a model that will never be able to say where a sentence came from.
