CBP Is Spending $1.7 Billion to Fence a Park With 1.3% of Border Crossings. Big Bend Sees Less Illegal Traffic Than a Typical Walmart Parking Lot Sees Shoplifting.
Bulldozers cleared brush in Big Bend National Park this week. Crossings there fell 74% last year.
Truths — What Happened

Bulldozers Showed Up in Big Bend Before the Public Meeting Did. Terlingua resident Natalie Newman filmed the moment contractors moved heavy machinery into the park near Santa Elena Canyon in early August, one of the most photographed spots in West Texas (CBS News). Residents had spent the year trying to head this off with a petition that gathered more than 175,000 signatures, a trip to Austin and Washington, and weekly protests, and it wasn't enough (CBS News). CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott says what's underway is survey work, not wall construction, and insists there's no 30-foot barrier planned inside the park itself (The Independent). What is planned, by his own agency's account, is 200 miles of patrol roads, 17 miles of vehicle barriers, and surveillance towers, funded by a $1.7 billion contract already awarded to Southwest Valley Constructors of Albuquerque (Houston Public Media). Redford river guide Charlie Angell says the road work alone will end his business, since it cuts through the same terrain a wall would (CBS News).

74%
That's how much illegal crossings in the Big Bend sector fell last year, before a single bulldozer arrived.

That drop is the number nobody defending the project has explained away. Big Bend Sector apprehensions accounted for just 1.3% of all southern border crossings in 2025, and inside the park itself, arrests have run 100 to 200 a year since 2023, according to the advocacy group Keep Big Bend Wild (Houston Public Media). Terrell County Sheriff Thaddeus Cleveland, a Republican and former Border Patrol agent, put it plainly on social media: "if the facts on the ground showed a need for a barrier here, I would support it. They don't" (The Independent). Bob Krumenaker, who used to run the park as its superintendent, says the brush already cleared near Santa Elena Canyon was riparian forest home to the yellow-billed cuckoo, a federally threatened bird (Houston Public Media).

The Same Contracts Are Closing a Door 1,200 Miles West. In Arizona, DHS contractors have finished 56 miles of new wall and 48 miles of secondary fencing since 2025, some of it doubling up into two 30-foot walls side by side (The New York Times). That construction sits across three of the four remaining wildlife corridors jaguars use to move between Sonora and Arizona, just as researcher Ganesh Marín confirmed the first native female jaguar seen near the border in more than 70 years. A male jaguar named Cinco has been roaming southern Arizona alone, with everything he needs except a mate the wall may now keep out.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The next fight isn't over whether the wall gets built. It's over who gets to say what it actually is.

Cornyn's Letter Will Get an Answer, and the Answer Will Change Nothing. Markwayne Mullin's office will almost certainly respond to Cornyn within the next two weeks with a version of what Commissioner Scott has already said publicly, that this is survey and design work, not wall construction (The Independent). That answer will not include a community meeting, because CBP has had months to hold one and hasn't, a gap Charlie Angell has already flagged as the actual problem (CBS News). The most likely outcome is that Cornyn, a lame duck with nothing left to trade for leverage, gets a courtesy reply and the contractors keep working, because the letter was never a mechanism that could stop a contract already awarded to Southwest Valley Constructors. Watch instead for Sheriff Cleveland, who still has a badge and a constituency, to be the one DHS eventually has to negotiate with directly.

That gap between what officials say and what's visible on the ground will almost certainly widen before it narrows. Scott's insistence that bulldozers near Santa Elena Canyon are clearing land for "patrol roads," not a wall, will keep running into the plain fact that Natalie Newman and other residents can film the equipment and the man camps themselves (CBS News). Expect CBP to keep drawing a hard verbal line between "vehicle barrier" and "wall" while doing very little to distinguish the two in practice, since Mullin himself has already muddied that line by telling Congress two miles will get a physical barrier while the rest gets "smart fence" (The Independent). The most likely near-term flashpoint is Laiken Jordahl's warning about the Rio Grande itself, since 17 miles of vehicle barriers in a park built around a river will almost certainly cut off boat put-ins that guides like Angell depend on within the current construction season.

The jaguar corridor closure points toward a slower but more permanent version of the same pattern.

It totally deflates the narrative that female jaguars are over 100 miles to the south.

Eamon Harrity, researcher, Sky Island Alliance

With Adela's presence confirmed and Cinco still alone in Arizona, the most consequential near-term test isn't political, it's biological: whether any jaguar attempts the crossing before the Arizona segments now under construction close the fourth remaining corridor. Wildlife biologists monitoring trail cameras at Cuenca Los Ojos will almost certainly be watching for exactly that movement over the coming months, aware that federal contractors, not conservationists, control the timeline now.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The fight so far has been about roads and barriers. The next one might be about who gets to decide what "finished" means.

Cleveland Could Become the Off-Ramp Nobody in Washington Has to Vote For. The Probabilities section already flagged Terrell County Sheriff Thaddeus Cleveland as the actor DHS will eventually have to deal with directly, since he has local standing that Cornyn, as a lame duck, no longer does. What's newly reachable is the specific shape that negotiation could take: Cleveland is a Republican, a former Border Patrol agent, and an elected sheriff who has already said publicly that the barrier isn't justified by the facts on the ground (The Independent). That combination didn't exist as a lever until this week, because until construction actually started, his objection was theoretical. Now that bulldozers are visible near Santa Elena Canyon, Cleveland has something Cornyn's letter didn't: a constituency watching in real time and a badge that makes him harder for DHS to dismiss as an activist. If Mullin's office wants a face-saving way to scale back the 200 miles of patrol roads without admitting the project was oversized from the start, negotiating a county-line compromise with Cleveland, framed as "local input," is the one path that lets DHS claim responsiveness while Commissioner Scott keeps insisting nothing changed.

That kind of local carve-out would only be possible because Cleveland is already inside the tent Cornyn's letter is not.

A Documented Species Discovery Could Force the Corridor Fight Into Court. Ganesh Marín's confirmation of a female jaguar near the border, combined with construction actively closing three of four wildlife corridors, opens a door that wasn't available before the sighting was verified this year. The Endangered Species Act requires federal agencies to consult with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service before actions that jeopardize a listed species' recovery, and jaguars have been listed as endangered since 1997. Until Adela and the other cats at Cuenca Los Ojos were confirmed, DHS could argue jaguar recovery in Arizona was speculative, a male alone with no mate in sight. That argument is harder to make now that researchers like Eamon Harrity have documented a breeding-age female close enough to reach Cinco. Conservation groups already active in the Big Bend fight, including the Center for Biological Diversity, whose Laiken Jordahl has been vocal about the Rio Grande access issue, have the standing and the track record to bring exactly this kind of challenge in Arizona. A lawsuit wouldn't stop the wall segments already finished. It could freeze the fourth corridor before contractors close it, which is the only piece of this story where the door hasn't shut yet.

A Joint Community Meeting Could Still Happen, and It Would Cost DHS Almost Nothing. Angell's complaint that CBP hasn't held a single public meeting is not, on its own, a lever. But it becomes one now that Cornyn has put a formal letter on the record and Cleveland has publicly broken with the administration's framing. Those two things together, a Republican senator's letter and a Republican sheriff's public dissent, give Mullin's office cover to hold the meeting Angell has been asking for all year without looking like it's caving to protesters. It would cost DHS nothing it hasn't already conceded rhetorically, since Scott keeps insisting the agency is "working to preserve the landscape" anyway. Whether that meeting happens is the clearest test of whether this administration treats transparency as a genuine option or a talking point, because the political cover to hold one exists for the first time since construction began.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The lie isn't the wall. It's the word "wall" itself, and who gets to decide what counts as one.

"We Are Not Building a 30-Foot Wall Through Big Bend." Commissioner Rodney Scott has said this three times now, most recently framing bulldozers near Santa Elena Canyon as "survey and design work" that happens to require heavy machinery, man camps, and staging areas for dozens of vehicles (The Independent). It's a claim built to survive a fact-check, because it's technically true. There will not be a 30-foot steel wall inside the park boundary. What there will be, by CBP's own description, is 200 miles of patrol road and 17 miles of vehicle barrier cut through the same terrain a wall would require, which is exactly what Natalie Newman meant when she said the destruction needed for the roads is "almost as much" as a vertical wall would take (CBS News). Scott's sentence answers a question nobody in Terlingua asked. Residents never said the fear was a specific height of steel. They said the fear was bulldozers in a riparian forest, road cuts along the Rio Grande, and access roads that end a river guide's livelihood, and all three of those things are happening under a label that technically isn't "wall."

The mechanism is a definitional shell game, and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin has already tipped how it works by telling Congress that two miles get a "physical barrier" while the rest gets "smart fence," a distinction that exists on a spreadsheet and nowhere on the ground crews are actually clearing.

Washington doesn't get to contradict what we can see with our own eyes.

No Big Bend Wall, advocacy group

That's the whole case in nine words. The believers here aren't gullible. They're responding to a genuine and reasonable fear, that the federal government spent a year insisting there'd be no wall in a national park and then sent contractors in without the community meeting Charlie Angell has been requesting since spring. The claim gives the administration something valuable: a defensible headline. "No 30-foot wall in Big Bend" is a sentence Scott can repeat on the CBP website indefinitely, and it will read as true in every fact-check, because it is true, in the narrowest possible sense of the word "wall." What it buys is time and cover, letting Southwest Valley Constructors keep working on a $1.7 billion contract while the political fight stays stuck arguing about a height nobody is actually building. It's the same maneuver a previous administration ran with drone strikes it called "targeted," where the technical accuracy of the label did the work of making the underlying thing sound smaller than it was.
