A Judge in Boston Just Cleared 5,000 Ethiopians for Deportation. A Miami Restroom Attendant Already Lost His Job to the Same Ruling.
Federal courts have now cleared TPS terminations for 13 nations, over 1 million people affected nationwide.
Truths — What Happened

Judge Brian Murphy Closes the Last Door. On Tuesday, Murphy removed the final injunction blocking the Trump administration from ending Temporary Protected Status for Ethiopians, the last of 13 nationalities still fighting termination in court (The Independent). More than 5,000 Ethiopians now have 60 days to leave the country or face deportation, even though the State Department still rates Ethiopia a level-three travel risk for kidnapping, terrorism, and landmines (Al Jazeera Online; The New Republic). DHS General Counsel James Percival celebrated on social media within hours: "All TPS terminations are in effect!" (The Independent). Murphy did leave one door open. He ruled plaintiffs can still argue in court that DHS acted out of racial or national-origin animus, a Fifth Amendment claim that keeps the Ethiopia case alive even as the deportation clock starts running (The Independent).

Ethiopia was the last case standing, but the pattern was set weeks earlier in Florida, where the termination already has a body count of lost jobs. When Haiti's TPS ended July 27, over 300,000 people lost protection, and the effects showed up first in the industries nobody notices until they're understaffed (Miami Herald). A 70-year-old man from Port-au-Prince who cleaned restrooms at Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport for 19 years, working the 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. shift five nights a week, had his badge voided in July (Miami Herald). He is one of 132 Haitian TPS holders who worked at FLL alone, and one of roughly 52,000 across Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach counties, concentrated in cooking, stocking, housekeeping, and security (Miami Herald). Wilkinson Sejour, who has owned Little Haiti's Chef Creole since 1992, says the policy he voted for in 2024 may now force him to close two of his seven restaurants (Miami Herald). The same story is repeating in nursing homes, where operators are scrambling to fill vacancies left by Haitian home-care workers since late July (The Wall Street Journal).

That gap between courtroom ruling and kitchen floor is also where enforcement is landing on people the government itself has vetted. In Houston, ICE detained Pastor Luis Morales Pérez at the airport after Venezuela's TPS ended, despite valid work authorization and no criminal record cited by the agency (Houston Chronicle). In New York, City Council data analyst Rafael Rubio spent five months detained after DHS called him "a criminal illegal alien," only for an immigration judge to grant him asylum, an outcome DHS is now appealing (CBS News).

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The pastor and the data analyst were vetted by the government's own process and detained anyway. That's not a bug in the system anymore. It's the design.

Ethiopians Have 60 Days. Most Will Spend Them in a Legal No-Man's-Land, Not on a Plane. Murphy's ruling started a clock, but the most likely next step isn't a wave of deportation flights to Addis Ababa. It's a repeat of what happened with Somalia, Venezuela, and Haiti: DHS revokes work authorization first, employers run e-Verify checks, and people lose jobs weeks before ICE ever knocks. Expect Florida, Minnesota, and the D.C. metro area, the three biggest Ethiopian population centers, to see the first wave of terminated work permits within the 60-day window, followed by the same kind of quiet attrition Miami-Dade saw with Haitian hospitality workers. The Fifth Amendment claim Murphy left alive will almost certainly not produce an injunction before that window closes. Courts have not paused a single termination on animus grounds so far this year, and Judge Burroughs is handling the nearly identical Somalia claim on a similar timeline with no signs of urgency.

That legal slow-walk is the mechanism, and it's about to repeat itself somewhere with sharper political consequences.

The Real Fight Moves to Congress, and November Is the Deadline That Matters. With courts no longer a viable check, Houston immigration attorney Gordon Quan's read is the accurate one: the only remaining lever is legislative, and it doesn't exist until Democrats hold both chambers (Houston Public Media). That makes South Florida's District 27 primary a preview, not a footnote. Both Democratic contenders, Eliott Rodriguez and Robin Peguero, have made TPS restoration part of their pitch against Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar, who currently holds a 20-point cushion (CBS News). The most likely outcome in November is that TPS becomes a live wedge issue in exactly the districts where the labor shortage is already visible, Miami-Dade, Broward, and the Nassau County races Trump is currently touring to shore up (NY1 News). Whether that translates into the two-thirds majority needed to override a veto is a different question entirely, and the honest answer is it almost certainly won't happen this cycle.

Which leaves the economic pressure to do what the courts and Congress currently can't.

Miami's Labor Shortage Will Get Worse Before Any Business Owner Gets Relief. Wilkinson Sejour's math, two of seven restaurants closing, will not stay a Chef Creole problem. The same 52,000-person hospitality and care workforce hollowed out in South Florida is about to lose its Ethiopian counterpart in whatever cities absorbed that community, and nursing home operators already scrambling from the Haitian termination will be the first to feel a second wave layer on top of the first (The Wall Street Journal). DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin will almost certainly frame every new vacancy as proof the policy is working as intended, not as a cost. The businesses absorbing that cost have no lobby built for this fight, and the workers replacing lost staff, if they materialize at all, will not show up in the next two weeks. What shows up in the next two weeks is more of what already showed up in Houston and New York: people the government vetted, then detained anyway, while the paperwork catches up to the politics.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

Rubio's asylum grant is one door. Sejour's second restaurant closing is another. Both were already open before this week — what's new is what sits next to them now.

A Fifth Amendment Claim in Boston Just Got a Companion Case in Somalia, and That's Enough for a Real Discovery Fight. Murphy kept the animus claim alive for Ethiopians. Burroughs is sitting on the nearly identical claim for Somalis, a case built partly on Trump's own recorded description of Somalis as "garbage" and "low IQ people" (Reuters). Two judges, two nearly identical Fifth Amendment theories, both still open. That's a new combination: parallel litigation in the same courthouse, same legal theory, different plaintiffs, which means discovery in one case can surface documents useful in the other. If Somalia plaintiffs get DHS internal deliberations into the record — emails, drafts, the kind of paper trail that shows whether "conditions have improved" was the real reason or the assigned one — that evidence doesn't stay contained to Somalia. It becomes exhibit material for Ethiopia, and for whichever of the other 11 terminated nationalities still has a live case. Percival's own social media habit, publicly declaring victory the moment each ruling drops, is the kind of contemporaneous statement plaintiffs' lawyers build timelines around. None of this stops a deportation clock. But it's the first mechanism in eight months of losses that could force DHS to explain itself under oath rather than in a press release.

That's a courtroom door. The one behind it is electoral, and it only opens if the first one produces something embarrassing enough to travel.

If Discovery Produces a Paper Trail, Salazar's District Becomes the Test of Whether TPS Can Actually Move a Republican Seat. Rodriguez and Peguero are already running on TPS restoration in a district Salazar won by 20 points. That's a soft opening, not a real threat, on its own. But layer in what Boston or Somalia discovery might surface, and layer in the fact that Salazar represents the same Miami-Dade zip codes where Chef Creole is down staff and airport concessions are short-handed. A Republican member of Congress whose own restaurant-owning constituents voted for the policy and are now watching it cost them workers is a different kind of vulnerable than an abstract national poll number. Salazar has cultivated a moderate-on-immigration brand for years, which means she has more room to break from the administration than most House Republicans, and also more exposure if she doesn't. The combination that wasn't reachable a month ago: documented evidence of DHS animus, sitting next to a hospitality-sector labor shortage, sitting next to a competitive primary already fighting to define the issue before November.

There's a fourth door in the room, and it's the one that doesn't require anyone losing.

Mamdani's Direct Line to Trump on Haitian TPS Is a Model City Halls Elsewhere Could Actually Copy. Mamdani told Trump directly that ending TPS for Haitians mattered to New York, and got nothing on the record back except a spokeswoman's line about restoring TPS to its "original intent" (NY1 News). No policy moved. But the mechanism is now demonstrated: a mayor can get a direct conversation with the president over TPS, and the city council can back it with real money, the $82 million New York has already committed to legal services for detained residents (CBS News). That's a template other city halls with large TPS populations, Houston, Miami-Dade, Broward, could adopt without waiting for Congress or the courts. It doesn't reverse a termination. It does mean the next Rubio case, the next data analyst or pastor detained despite a clean record, has a funded legal defense and a mayor's office willing to make noise before the five-month clock runs out, not after.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Percival called it a win for the rule of law. The rule he's citing didn't say what he claims it says.

"TPS Was Never Meant to Be Permanent" Is Being Used to Erase Why It Existed in the First Place. DHS General Counsel James Percival and Secretary Markwayne Mullin have built the entire public case for these terminations on a single word: temporary (Houston Public Media). Mullin put it bluntly after the Ethiopia ruling, warning anyone whose status lapsed to leave immediately or be swiftly deported (Al Jazeera Online). It's an appealing argument precisely because it sounds like a return to the rules rather than an abandonment of them, and it answers a real anxiety: that immigration categories quietly become permanent without anyone voting on it. But the statute was never written to expire on a clock. It was written to expire when the underlying danger did, and DHS's own December announcement conceded the opposite about Ethiopia, arguing conditions had improved enough for safe return while the State Department kept the country at a level-three travel advisory for kidnapping, terrorism, and landmines (Al Jazeera Online). Haiti tells the same story in reverse. The country's president was assassinated in 2021, the conditions cited to justify TPS in the first place, and gang kidnapping has since made it dangerous enough that Bloomberg found human-rights advocates calling it the kidnapping capital of the Caribbean, not the description of a country ready to reabsorb 330,000 returnees (Bloomberg Business). Venezuela's protections ended the same way, over earthquake damage that never got repaired, which is how a Seventh-day Adventist pastor in Houston with valid work authorization and no criminal record ended up detained at an airport with his wife and children waiting (Houston Chronicle).

Temporary Protected Status is exactly that -- temporary.

ICE, statement on the detention of Pastor Luis Morales Pérez

The word is doing the work the evidence won't. Who benefits from that substitution is not abstract. DHS gets a one-line justification that requires no country-by-country accounting of actual danger, which is why the Fifth Amendment claims Judge Murphy and Judge Burroughs both kept alive exist at all: plaintiffs argue the "temporary" framing was applied selectively, to Black and Brown-majority nations, while the underlying conditions were treated as irrelevant (The New Republic). Politically, the framing lets the administration claim rule-of-law discipline while producing outcomes, a father of two detained at his gate, a City Council data analyst held five months before an immigration judge granted him asylum, that look nothing like rule-following and everything like a policy working exactly as intended. The men writing the press releases will not be the ones filling the vacancy at Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International. The word "temporary" costs DHS nothing. It has already cost Wilkinson Sejour two restaurants and an untold number of people currently deciding whether boarding a flight home is safer than staying to fight a case they may not win.
