Donald Trump Jr. Told GOP Officials to Drop Prediction Market Lawsuits. He's Paid More Than $300,000 by One of the Companies He Was Defending.
Trump Jr. pitched Republican attorneys general in March to back off Kalshi and Polymarket oversight.
Truths — What Happened

In March, at a three-day retreat at the Ritz-Carlton in New Orleans, Donald Trump Jr. sat onstage with Montana's attorney general and told a room of Republican state officials that they were being misled (The New York Times). The real threat, he said, wasn't unregulated betting markets. It was traditional gambling companies trying to protect their "monopolies" by attacking a rival product (CNBC). Prediction markets, he told them, already had robust oversight, just not from the states in that room.

Trump Jr. is paid by the industry he was defending. Kalshi hired him as an adviser last year, part of a package worth more than $300,000 in company shares that have since multiplied in value (The New York Times). He also sits on Polymarket's advisory board and holds a stake in the company through his investment firm, 1789 Capital. He has said publicly, through a spokesman, that he "does not interface with the federal government on behalf of any company he invests in or advises" (Mediaite). The New Orleans pitch was not to the federal government. It was to the states trying to regulate around it.

That distinction is the fight itself. Twenty states, red and blue, are suing over whether prediction markets answer to state sports-betting law or federal commodities law, and 44 states signed a letter last month calling the platforms a "new form of casino" targeting young people (The New York Times). The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the small federal agency Trump Jr. told the room could handle it, had never sued a state in its 50-year history until this year. It has now sued nine, all led by Democratic governors, and twice invoked emergency powers to let prediction markets sidestep court orders (The New Republic).

what the Tax Foundation estimates states lose annually if prediction markets stay outside state tax and gambling law.

Kalshi didn't deny the lobbying. It posted a defense of it on its own website, comparing state efforts to shut it down to North Carolina trying to shut down the stock market, and confirmed that its lobbyist, Jim Harrell, helped write the language of a North Carolina budget provision that taxes prediction markets at 6% against sportsbooks' 23% (CNBC). Rob Schwartz, the CFTC's own general counsel until last year, put it plainly.

A lot of money is at stake.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The room in New Orleans wasn't the only audience Trump Jr. was playing to. The next few weeks will show whether the pitch worked.

The nine state lawsuits will almost certainly become a Supreme Court case within the next year, and the White House will file in support of the industry. The CFTC's break from a half-century of not suing states was not a one-off enforcement choice. It was a legal strategy built to force a jurisdictional ruling, and every emergency-powers invocation since has been a rehearsal for that argument. The most likely next filing to watch is Arizona's criminal case against Kalshi, the first of its kind, which will almost certainly draw a federal preemption challenge within the next two weeks. If the CFTC wins that framing, all nine state suits collapse into one federal question, and Kalshi and Polymarket get exactly what Trump Jr. asked for in New Orleans without ever having to win a single state legislature.

That outcome depends on the states staying divided, which is the more fragile assumption. Forty-four states signed a joint letter calling prediction markets a casino, but joint letters are cheap and litigation is not. The most likely fracture point is North Carolina, where Kalshi's own lobbyist already shaped the tax language that let the platform undercut sportsbooks by 17 percentage points. Watch for two or three more red states to quietly adopt similar carve-outs in the next legislative cycle, not because they've been persuaded prediction markets are safe, but because a 6% cut of a growing market beats a 23% cut of nothing once the federal courts start looking likely to win.

Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes will almost certainly face pressure from her own party's donor base to settle rather than litigate to a Supreme Court loss. Democratic attorneys general are the ones actually named as defendants in the CFTC's nine suits, which makes them the ones who'll pay the legal bill for a fight the industry increasingly looks positioned to win. The most likely near-term move is a settlement offer from one of the nine, probably from a smaller state without the budget for a multiyear appeal, sometime in the next month. If that happens, Kalshi will publicize it the way it publicized its own lobbying memo on its website, as proof the states never had a real case rather than proof they ran out of money to make one.

The through-line is the same one the CFTC's own former general counsel put a name to. Whoever wins this fight decides whether prediction markets are a regulated financial product or an unregulated casino, and Trump Jr.'s stake in the outcome does not require him to touch a single federal lever to benefit from it.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

If the states fracture the way North Carolina already has, the fight stops being about prediction markets at all — it becomes about who else in Trump's orbit gets to write their own tax code.

A state attorney general breaks ranks publicly, and Kalshi's lobbying memo becomes the template other industries ask for by name. North Carolina's carve-out only became visible because Kalshi chose to publish its own lobbyist's fingerprints, a strange flex unless the point was advertising. If one more state follows that path in the next legislative session, the combination that opens is bigger than prediction markets. Every industry currently losing a federal preemption fight, cannabis, crypto exchanges, gig-work platforms, has watched a company write favorable tax language into a state budget and then brag about it rather than bury it. That's a new playbook, not just a new precedent. It says the fastest way to beat a 44-state coalition isn't to fight all 44. It's to buy the first defector and let the letter's moral weight do the rest of the work collapsing on its own.

That defection is only thinkable because the CFTC already showed states losing costs money, not just leverage. Which makes the next door visible only from inside this one.

A cash-strapped state settles with the CFTC before Arizona's case even reaches a preemption ruling, and that settlement becomes the CFTC's evidence exhibit in every other suit. The nine sued states are already the ones paying to defend a fight the industry looks positioned to win. A settlement from the smallest of them wouldn't need to concede anything about the merits to still function as proof, in Kalshi's hands, that the other eight are just slower to fold. Kalshi has already shown it prefers publishing over lawyering when the fact pattern helps it. A settlement gives it a second memo to post, this time with a Democratic attorney general's own signature on it, which does more damage to the 44-state coalition's unity than anything the CFTC could win in court.

The version of this that doesn't end in erosion runs through the same room, differently used.

The retreat where Trump Jr. made his pitch becomes the model for a bipartisan attorneys general coalition that regulates prediction markets without waiting on Congress or the Supreme Court. The Ritz-Carlton gathering was a closed-door, multiday retreat, exactly the format that let 44 states coordinate a joint letter in the first place. Nothing about that infrastructure belongs to one party. If a handful of red-state attorneys general who heard Trump Jr.'s pitch directly decide the "vested interest" framing doesn't survive contact with a $2 billion revenue hole in their own budgets, the same retreat structure that Trump Jr. used to ask for retreat could instead produce a model state statute, drafted jointly across party lines, that several states adopt at once rather than picking off state by state. Rob Schwartz spent a career watching the CFTC regulate commodities markets nobody fights over. A coordinated state framework wouldn't need federal permission to exist. It would just need the states to stop waiting for the Supreme Court to tell them what North Carolina's legislature already figured out on its own.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

The New Orleans pitch wasn't a private aside. It was a story someone wanted repeated, which means the story is worth taking apart.

"States are being misled by gambling companies protecting their monopolies." That's the claim Trump Jr. made to the room, and it's the frame Kalshi has repeated since, comparing state regulation to North Carolina trying to shut down the stock market. It's a believable story if you're a Republican attorney general who's spent a career skeptical of new industries getting strangled by entrenched incumbents lobbying through the back door. Traditional casino operators really are pushing states to act, really have hired Chris Christie to make their case on television, and really do stand to lose customers to a cheaper, less-regulated competitor. The lie isn't that casinos have an interest. It's the "only." Nine state suits and 44-state letters aren't a lobbying campaign dressed up as consumer protection. They're a response to platforms that took bets on how many homes would burn during the Eaton and Palisades fires, that Kalshi's own referrals suggest are catching dozens of possible insider traders a quarter, and that a CFTC operating at its thinnest staffing in two decades has brought all of three civil cases over. The states suing aren't defending a monopoly. They're defending a tax base and, in Arizona's case, bringing the only criminal charge anyone has filed over it.

Who benefits from the monopoly framing is not abstract. Trump Jr. is paid by the company he's defending, and the framing does something specific: it recasts a jurisdictional fight over who taxes and polices a booming industry as a fight between good disruption and bad incumbents, a story built for an audience that already distrusts entrenched interests on instinct. It also gives Republican attorneys general a face-saving reason to drop a fight that costs money to keep fighting. Nobody has to admit the industry lobbied them out of a $2 billion problem. They can say they were protecting consumers from casino cronyism instead.

It's as if North Carolina wanted to shut down the stock market.

Kalshi, company statement

The tell is in what Kalshi didn't dispute. It confirmed its own lobbyist wrote the tax carve-out that undercuts sportsbooks by 17 percentage points in North Carolina, then framed the disclosure as transparency rather than the thing the "monopoly" story was supposed to distract from. Andrew Jackson rewarded loyalists with jobs once he'd won. This is the same instinct with better lawyers: the spoils system doesn't need a uniform anymore, just an advisory board seat and a story about who the real monopolist is.
