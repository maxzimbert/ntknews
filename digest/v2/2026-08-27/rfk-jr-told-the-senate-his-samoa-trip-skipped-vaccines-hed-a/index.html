RFK Jr. Told the Senate His Samoa Trip Skipped Vaccines. He'd Already Mailed the Prime Minister Asking to Study the MMR Shot.
Documents show Kennedy wrote Samoa's leader about vaccines eight times before denying it under questioning.
Truths — What Happened

A 2019 Letter Just Caught Up With a 2025 Confirmation Hearing. Robert F. Kennedy Jr. told Sen. Ron Wyden he went to Samoa for "nothing to do with vaccines," and told Sen. Edward Markey the next day the same thing, word for word (The Independent). But a letter he sent Samoa's prime minister in January 2019, obtained by the AP and The Guardian, used the words "vaccine" or "vaccination" eight times and proposed a "health informatics assessment" of what happened with the country's MMR shots after two infant deaths (PBS). In the letter he floated a specific theory — that the deaths were tied to bad vaccine manufacturing lots — the exact claim his own anti-vaccine nonprofit was pushing on the island at the time (The New Republic). HHS's answer was not a correction. It was a legal threat, calling any suggestion that Kennedy misled Congress "not only false but outright defamatory" (The Independent).

What happened after that letter is the part Samoa never got past. Kennedy's visit preceded a collapse in the country's vaccination rate, from roughly 60-70% down to 31%, followed by 5,707 measles cases and 83 deaths, most of them children under five, in a nation that hadn't seen an outbreak that size since 1985 (The New Republic).

The pattern didn't stay in the Pacific. Five years before Pennsylvania's own outbreak, Kennedy stood on a farm in Lancaster County and told 1,500 people at a fair that health agencies wanted the Amish to vaccinate because "they cannot stand the fact that you are healthy," and joked that his own childhood measles meant a week off from school (The New York Times). Measles vaccination among Lancaster County kindergartners has since dropped to 88% from 94% (The New York Times). That county is now home to the largest measles outbreak in the country, and this week it recorded Pennsylvania's first measles deaths in 35 years, including a newborn (Ars Technica).

Kennedy's response to that death wasn't caution. It was suspicion. He posted on his official X account that the deaths "may even have been altogether fabricated by one of the Governor's hopeful staffers," pointing at Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, who has criticized his record (Ars Technica). The Lancaster County coroner had already stated the newborn died of a ruptured spleen and that a pathologist didn't attribute it to measles, but vaccine expert Paul Offit, who said he'd been in direct contact with state officials on the case, noted that measles is a documented cause of spleen rupture in newborns and asked the coroner to name an alternative if not that (Ars Technica).

All it takes is somebody like that, an anti-vaxxer, to make a personal appearance to a group who already has second thoughts about immunizations.

Dr. Alan Peterson, Lancaster County family physician
Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

Kennedy's fabrication claim now has to survive contact with a coroner's office, a governor's press shop, and a case count that keeps climbing — none of which are inclined to let it go quietly.

Josh Shapiro's office will almost certainly escalate past a statement. A sitting governor accused by a Cabinet secretary of fabricating infant deaths for political gain does not let that sit for a news cycle, especially one already positioning himself as a national Kennedy critic. The most likely next step, inside the next 3-5 days, is a formal demand for retraction or a request that HHS's inspector general review Kennedy's public statements, paired with Shapiro amplifying the Lancaster County coroner's account to box Kennedy into either producing evidence for the fabrication claim or dropping it. Pennsylvania Republicans watching the outbreak spread past Lancaster County have little incentive to defend Kennedy's version once the death count and case count keep moving in the same direction; Josh Parsons, the county commissioner Kennedy leaned on for the "zero deaths" line, is the one left holding a claim the coroner has already undercut.

That leaves the congressional half of the story, which will move slower and land softer. Democrats like Wyden and Markey, the same senators Kennedy told "nothing to do with vaccines" on consecutive days, will almost certainly send formal letters or demand testimony within the next two weeks, using the Samoa letters as the evidentiary hook. But a hearing requires a Republican-controlled committee to schedule one, and nothing in Kennedy's record with this Congress suggests that happens.

That number is the real leverage, not the letters. The most probable outcome over the next two weeks is that the case count keeps rising in Pennsylvania and spreads to at least one additional state cluster, which will force HHS to keep responding publicly even as Kennedy avoids the perjury question entirely. Watch whether HHS's $8.5 million response effort, mentioned only in passing so far, gets a public dollar figure update or gets quietly expanded — that's the tell for whether internal pressure at the agency is starting to outweigh Kennedy's instinct to fight the coroner instead of the virus. The losers if this trajectory holds are straightforward: Lancaster County parents watching vaccination rates keep falling, and Shapiro's credibility if he escalates and HHS simply outlasts him with silence, the same strategy that worked on the Samoa letters.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The room Kennedy is standing in has a coroner's report, a governor with a national platform, and a Republican Congress that hasn't moved — but two of those three things just became combinable in a way they weren't last week.

A state attorney general could open the door federal prosecutors won't walk through. The probabilities section already laid out why a congressional perjury referral is a dead end under this House and Senate — Republicans aligned with Trump have no incentive to schedule that hearing. But Pennsylvania's own attorney general doesn't need Congress. Shapiro was a state AG himself before he was governor, and Pennsylvania's current AG answers to the same political map Shapiro is building. The newborn's death, the coroner's finding, and Kennedy's public "fabricated" accusation now sit in the same jurisdiction as Lancaster County — which means a state-level inquiry into whether a Cabinet secretary knowingly made false public statements about a specific, named death is newly reachable in a way a Senate referral never was. That's not a hypothetical instrument; state AGs have opened inquiries into public officials' statements about specific deaths before. What's new is that Kennedy handed one a death, a denial, and a paper trail in the same week.

That combination — a documented death plus a Cabinet official's public claim about it — is also what makes the next door visible.

If Pennsylvania moves, other states with active outbreaks get a template, not just sympathy. This only opens if the first door does. Right now, other state health departments dealing with falling vaccination rates have been reactive — responding to case counts, not to Kennedy's public statements about those cases. But an AG inquiry in Pennsylvania would be the first instance of a state institution treating Kennedy's rhetoric itself as the liability, not just the virus. That's a template other governors facing their own outbreaks could borrow within weeks, not months, especially any who watched Shapiro's move and have their own coroners sitting on inconvenient findings. The mechanism that makes this reachable now and not earlier: Kennedy's own past pattern, from Samoa to Lancaster County, gives any state AG a ready-made timeline instead of a one-off statement to evaluate in isolation.

The version of this that doesn't end in more casualties runs through Paul Offit, not Shapiro. Offit is already on record contradicting Kennedy with the state officials he's been in direct contact with — he's not a political actor, which is exactly why his credibility travels further than Shapiro's. The newly reachable move is a coalition of pediatric infectious disease specialists, the kind of people HHS still nominally employs, issuing a joint clinical statement on measles-related infant mortality that doesn't mention Kennedy by name at all. That's the generative door: not a political fight Kennedy can reframe as persecution, but a clinical consensus document that quietly becomes the thing state health departments cite instead of HHS guidance. It doesn't require Kennedy to resign, retract, or even respond. It just requires doctors deciding they no longer need his agency's permission to tell parents the truth.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Kennedy has two lies running at once — one about a six-year-old trip, one about a six-day-old baby — and they share the same design.

"The Samoa trip had nothing to do with vaccines." Kennedy said this to Wyden and Markey on consecutive days during his confirmation, and HHS is still saying it now, calling any suggestion otherwise "outright defamatory" — a threat, not a rebuttal. A reader inclined to believe him has a reason to: it is easier to accept that a Cabinet secretary misspoke than to accept that he sat before the Senate and constructed a denial around a letter he'd written himself, one that used the word "vaccine" eight times and floated a specific manufacturing-defect theory to Samoa's prime minister. That's the appeal of the lie — it lets the confirmation process still mean something. But the letter predates the testimony by six years, which means this isn't a memory failing under pressure. It's a story built to survive a hearing, and it worked; skeptical Republican senators who needed cover to vote yes got exactly the sentence they needed to hear. The people who benefit are the same ones who needed him confirmed in the first place, an administration that got its health secretary installed on a claim now sitting next to a signed letter that contradicts it, line for line.

That lie bought Kennedy a Senate seat. The second one is buying him time on a body count.

"The newborn's death may have been fabricated." Kennedy posted this from his official HHS account, not a personal one, citing a county commissioner's tweet over the coroner's own office. The believable version of this claim leans on something real: the coroner did attribute the death to a ruptured spleen and did not, in his own statement, tie it to measles. Kennedy is technically citing an actual document. What he's leaving out is Paul Offit, the pediatric infectious disease physician who was in direct contact with the state officials the coroner wasn't, and who has laid out exactly how measles causes that same rupture in newborns.

Any claim or insinuation based on these letters that Secretary Kennedy lied under oath or to Congress is not only false but outright defamatory.

HHS, written statement

Kennedy doesn't need the fabrication claim to hold up in court. He needs it to hold up for a week, long enough to blunt a dead infant as evidence against the policies he's spent two decades building a following on. The people who benefit are the same anti-vaccine media ecosystem Kennedy pointed toward first — he linked The HighWire before the ink on the coroner's report was dry — an audience that has followed him from a Lancaster County fairground to a Cabinet office and needs him to still be right. Samoa's health ministry, after its own outbreak, eventually admitted what its vaccination collapse cost. Pennsylvania hasn't gotten that admission yet. It has a governor, a coroner, and a case count instead.
