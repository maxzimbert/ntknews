Amazon and Alphabet Are Each Other's Best AI Customers. That Loop Is Now Propping Up Both Stocks.

Amazon and Alphabet posted strong profits — fueled, in significant part, by selling AI services to each other.

Truths

The Profits Are Real. So Is the Circular Logic Beneath Them.

Amazon and Alphabet both reported strong earnings, and the AI buildout is getting the credit — cloud revenue up, data center demand surging, Wall Street satisfied (New York Times). That part is straightforward. The part that deserves a slower read is where the revenue is actually coming from. The two companies are, in meaningful measure, each other's biggest AI customers — Amazon buying Google Cloud services, Alphabet buying AWS infrastructure, both of them booking the transactions as growth. The money is real. The independence of the growth story is less so.

That's not fraud. It's not even unusual in a capital-intensive buildout — the railroad barons bought steel from each other's mills too. But it does mean the profit figures are doing double duty: they're both the evidence that AI is paying off and the mechanism that makes the evidence appear.

What Changes When the Loop Tightens

The practical consequence falls on anyone whose investment thesis depends on AI revenue being a signal from outside the industry — pension funds benchmarked to tech indices, retail investors who read the earnings headlines and concluded the AI moment had arrived, and the analysts whose price targets now have to account for the possibility that a slowdown at one company compresses revenue at the other simultaneously (New York Times). It's not contagion exactly. It's more like the difference between a building with independent load-bearing walls and one where the walls are also the floors.

Two companies
When the top two AI infrastructure sellers are also top customers of each other, "industry demand" and "intercompany billing" start to describe the same transaction.

The executives at both companies have every incentive to keep building — the capital expenditures are already committed, the data centers are already under construction, and stopping now would crater the story faster than slowing growth would. That's not a conspiracy. That's just how sunk costs work. The question for the next earnings cycle isn't whether profits are real. It's whether the customer base has grown beyond the companies building the infrastructure — or whether the boom is still, to a meaningful degree, billing itself.

Probabilities

The Next Earnings Cycle Will Answer the Question Truths Left Open.

Amazon and Alphabet's Q3 Reports Will Either Expand the Customer List or Expose It.

The most consequential thing that happens in the next 14 days isn't a policy announcement or a market correction — it's whether either company, in analyst calls or investor filings, names a meaningful new category of external AI customers: manufacturers, insurers, healthcare systems, logistics firms buying cloud infrastructure because their own operations require it. If that list has grown, the circular logic Truths documented is a growth phase, not a structural condition. If the customer list still reads like a roster of the same ten technology companies billing each other, the profit figures that satisfied Wall Street this quarter will start to look like the last strong quarter before the revision.

The actors most exposed to that outcome are the institutional funds that increased tech allocations after the earnings beat — pension managers who read cloud revenue growth as external validation. They almost certainly will not reduce exposure before the next filing; the lag between signal and reallocation is measured in quarters, not days. That means the period between now and Q3 results is the window where the thesis is most crowded and least tested.

The Capital Expenditure Commitments Will Lock Both Companies Into the Story Regardless.

The sunk-cost dynamic Truths named is also the mechanism most likely to determine what Amazon and Alphabet actually say next. Data center construction already underway doesn't pause because an analyst raises a circularity concern — it accelerates, because the cost of stopping exceeds the cost of continuing and hoping external demand materializes. That means both companies will almost certainly announce additional infrastructure investment in their next communications cycle, which will be reported as confidence in AI demand, which will make the loop harder to examine from the outside rather than easier.

The institutional winner in that scenario is the hyperscaler with the more diversified external customer base going into Q3. The loser is whichever one is still, by September, citing the other as a primary growth driver without a countervailing story. Analysts watching the revenue-mix disclosures — specifically the ratio of intercompany to third-party AI services revenue — will have the clearest read on whether the boom has a foundation beneath it or whether it's still, in the most literal accounting sense, billing itself forward.

Possibilities

The Scenarios Neither Earnings Call Will Address

If a Major External Customer Publicly Walks Back Its AI Commitment, the Feedback Loop Runs Backward.

The base case Probabilities mapped is orderly: Q3 either expands the customer list or doesn't, and the market adjusts with the usual quarter-lag. But there's a less comfortable version. If a large, visible external customer — a healthcare system, an insurer, a logistics firm that made a public bet on cloud AI — announces it's scaling back, pausing, or openly questioning ROI, it doesn't just remove revenue from one line item. It reframes every prior earnings beat as evidence of a different story than the one that was told. The executives at Amazon and Alphabet have the sunk-cost logic working in their favor right now; that same logic becomes a liability the moment the narrative shifts, because it means they kept building after the signal changed. The investors most exposed aren't the retail traders — they'll react late regardless. It's the institutional funds that justified their increased allocations to their own boards using the Q2 beat as cover. A public walkback by a named, recognizable company gives those boards a question their managers can't answer cleanly (New York Times).

The condition that makes this more than hypothetical: any major enterprise AI deployment that's been running long enough to generate internal performance data is now old enough to generate internal disappointment data too.

The More Hopeful Scenario Is Also the More Structural One.

Here's what almost nobody is considering: the circularity that looks like fragility could also be, under the right conditions, a forcing mechanism for genuine diversification. If Amazon and Alphabet's finance teams are watching the same intercompany revenue ratios that outside analysts are beginning to scrutinize — and they almost certainly are — the internal pressure to grow third-party revenue isn't just strategic, it's defensive. Companies don't like being the subject of a circularity story any more than their investors do.

One quarter
A single earnings cycle in which third-party AI services revenue demonstrably outpaces intercompany billing would do more to validate the AI buildout thesis than any number of infrastructure announcements — because it would mean the money is coming from outside the room.

That outcome requires that the enterprise customers already in pilots — the manufacturers, the healthcare systems, the logistics firms — find enough operational value to convert from experiment to contract at scale. It's not the most likely scenario Probabilities mapped. But it's the one that would make the boom real in the way the headlines have been implying it already is, and the conditions for it — massive infrastructure already built, competitive pricing pressure between hyperscalers, enterprises with genuine data problems looking for leverage — are all present. The gap between plausible and probable is narrower here than the circularity story suggests.

Lies

The Lies Circulating Around the AI Boom Aren't Random — They're Load-Bearing

"The Profits Prove the AI Moment Has Arrived."

This is the claim running through financial media, through brokerage newsletters, through the earnings-beat headlines that landed last week. The mechanism is simple: strong numbers appear, and the easiest explanation — AI is working, demand is real, the buildout was right — gets attached to them before anyone asks where the revenue originated. Amazon and Alphabet's investor relations teams aren't lying outright. They're letting a true statement do false work. Profits are up. What "profits are up" means, in this specific case, is doing considerably more lifting than the phrase can bear.

The people most likely to believe it are the ones with the most reasonable excuse to: retail investors who don't have access to revenue-mix disclosures, pension beneficiaries whose fund managers increased tech allocations after the Q2 beat and told them the AI thesis had been validated. The fear the story answers is the fear of missing it — of being the person who sat out the defining technology buildout of the decade because they were too cautious to act. That's not a foolish fear. It's the fear the framing is designed to activate.

The beneficiaries of the claim spreading are not the companies themselves, exactly — Amazon and Alphabet's stock prices would survive a more honest framing of the customer list. The beneficiaries are the analysts whose price targets depend on the AI revenue story remaining unexamined through at least one more cycle, and the institutional managers whose Q2 allocation decisions now need a Q3 rationale. Every week the circularity story stays out of the main earnings narrative is a week those positions don't require explaining to a board.

The tell is in what the coverage didn't ask: not whether AI revenue was growing, but whether the customers generating that growth existed independently of the companies reporting it. A boom in which the infrastructure sellers are also the infrastructure buyers produces real profits. It does not produce the external validation that "the AI moment has arrived" requires. That distinction isn't subtle. It's the whole question. The fact that it went largely unasked in the initial earnings cycle — that the headline wrote itself as confirmation rather than as something still to be confirmed — is the mechanism the lie runs on. Not fabrication. Framing. The story the numbers are allowed to tell before anyone checks the customer list.
