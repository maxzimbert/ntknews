France's Highest Court Just Struck Down Macron's Teen Social Media Ban. He's Sending It Back for a Rewrite.

France's Constitutional Council blocked a law banning social media for under-15s, citing freedom of expression.

Truths

Macron's Signature Effort to Keep Teenagers Off Social Media Didn't Survive Its Own Constitution

The French Constitutional Council struck down a bill that would have banned social media use for children under 15, ruling that the law infringed on constitutional free-expression protections (The Independent). Macron had made the legislation a visible priority. The court made it a visible problem. Within hours of the ruling, he ordered his prime minister to rework the bill rather than abandon it — which means the policy survives as an intention, but not yet as law (Techmeme / Reuters).

The losers in the short term are the teenagers the law was designed to protect, who remain unprotected while the government tries again. The winners, for now, are the platforms — Meta, TikTok, Snap — that would have faced the enforcement burden and had every incentive to see the bill die in court. They didn't even have to lobby against it. The Constitutional Council did the work.

The Ruling Reveals a Problem Every Democracy Is Running Into

That outcome isn't uniquely French. It's the same constitutional tension showing up in American courts, British parliamentary debates, and EU regulatory fights: governments want to wall children off from platforms that were designed by adults, for adults, optimized for engagement over wellbeing — and they keep reaching for blunt legislative instruments that their own legal frameworks won't hold (Al Jazeera). The free-expression problem isn't incidental. It's the core engineering challenge, and France just found out the hard way that a ban is not the same as a solution.

Macron's order to rework the bill rather than shelve it suggests he reads the ruling as a drafting problem, not a values problem (Guardian). He may be right. He may also be discovering that writing a law that restricts minors' platform access without touching adults' speech rights is harder than announcing one. The rewrite will show which it is.

Probabilities

The Rewrite Is Where Macron Finds Out If He Was Right

The revised bill will almost certainly narrow the restriction rather than abandon it — and that narrowing is where the platforms get their real victory.

The Constitutional Council didn't rule that protecting children from social media is unconstitutional. It ruled that this particular mechanism — a categorical age ban — swept too broadly across expression rights. That's a specific legal instruction, not a philosophical objection, and Macron's immediate order to his prime minister signals he heard it that way. The most likely outcome in the next two weeks is a revised bill that threads the needle: age-verification requirements or platform-side restrictions on algorithmic recommendation for under-15s, rather than a hard access ban. That version is harder to strike down on free-expression grounds. It's also far easier for Meta and TikTok to comply with on paper while routing around in practice. The platforms, having survived the blunter version, will now face the more negotiable one — which means they shift from litigation risk to lobbying mode, where they have historically performed much better.

That shift matters for every other country watching. The EU's Digital Services Act framework is already threading the same needle, and France's rewrite will function as a template — or a cautionary tale — for national governments trying to operate within it. The UK's Online Safety Act is at roughly the same implementation stage. If France's revised bill holds up constitutionally, those governments gain a usable model. If the rewrite is watered down enough to pass legal scrutiny but weak enough to be unenforceable, it will confirm the working assumption of the platforms' legal teams: that democracies can't write a law tight enough to actually constrain them.

Under-15s are the target population of a ban that has now been voided once and must clear constitutional review again before it applies to a single account
Every day the rewrite takes is another day the original problem runs unaddressed.

The harder probability is structural: Macron is betting that a drafting fix resolves a values conflict. The free-expression objection the Council raised isn't a technical glitch — it reflects a genuine constitutional tension between parental-state protection logic and individual liberty logic that no amount of careful wording fully dissolves. The prime minister's office will face pressure to produce a bill that looks strong for political purposes but survives judicial review, which usually produces something that does neither particularly well. If the rewrite clears the Council, the fight moves to enforcement — and that's where the platforms' compliance infrastructure, not their lawyers, becomes the decisive factor.

Possibilities

The scenarios no one in the prime minister's office is modeling yet

If the rewrite stalls long enough, the platforms may solve this problem themselves — and not in a way Macron would recognize as a solution.

Here's the thing almost nobody is considering: the legal limbo created by a struck-down ban, a rewrite in progress, and a second constitutional review still ahead may last long enough that Meta and TikTok move first. Not because they've turned generous, but because voluntary age-gating — announced loudly, implemented loosely — is the oldest play in platform lobbying. It generates favorable press, blunts legislative momentum, and produces compliance metrics that are genuinely difficult to audit from the outside. France's rewrite could end up ratifying a framework the platforms designed for themselves. The EU's Digital Services Act created exactly this opening: if platforms can demonstrate "appropriate measures," the threshold for additional national legislation rises (Al Jazeera). The condition that makes this likely isn't bad faith on Macron's part — it's that the rewrite takes until late autumn, the news cycle moves, and the political urgency that got the original bill passed doesn't fully regenerate for a second vote.

The more hopeful long shot: the Constitutional Council's ruling becomes the template that actually sticks.

The court didn't close the door. It specified what wouldn't fit through it. That's unusual legislative guidance — normally courts strike things down and leave reconstruction to politicians. If the prime minister's office treats the ruling as a design specification rather than a defeat, the revised bill could end up more durable than the original, precisely because it was forced to survive harder scrutiny before it became law (The Independent). The UK and Germany are both watching closely enough that a French bill that holds up becomes a model they can adopt without having to absorb the same constitutional collision first. That's a genuinely constructive path — narrow, but real. The condition is that the prime minister's office prioritizes legal architecture over political optics, which is not a condition that holds consistently in any democracy, France included.

What the headline buries is that "citing constitutional freedoms" is a gift, not just a rebuke — it names the constraint precisely enough that a careful drafter can work around it. Whether Macron's government is feeling careful or feeling rushed will determine which of these two possibilities actually arrives.

Lies

The Lie That Makes a Bad Law Sound Like a Rescue

"This Ruling Proves Courts Are Protecting Platforms Over Children."

The claim is circulating in French political commentary and has the structure of something that will travel: a constitutional court strikes down a child-protection law, and the people hurt are the children. Who benefits? The corporations. Who intervened on their behalf? The judges. The logic is clean enough to feel true before you examine it.

Macron's allies have the most to gain from it spreading. A government that wrote a law the court couldn't hold isn't a government that failed at drafting — it's a government that was thwarted by institutions doing Big Tech's bidding. That reframe converts a competence problem into a conspiracy, and it arrives pre-loaded with exactly the kind of grievance this political moment runs on: institutions that perform neutrality while serving money. The fear underneath it is real. Parents who've watched their children disappear into a screen for three years don't need much convincing that powerful forces are protecting the thing harming their kids. The lie doesn't invent that fear. It just redirects it away from the government that wrote a bill the Constitutional Council found constitutionally defective — and toward the court that said so.

The mechanism the lie conceals is straightforward. The Constitutional Council cited freedom of expression, a right that protects the children the law was meant to shield as much as it protects anyone else. A court ruling that a ban is too broad isn't a ruling that children don't matter — it's a ruling that the instrument was wrong. The platforms didn't argue the case. They didn't need to. The drafters handed them the outcome by reaching for the bluntest possible tool when the constitutional framework required a more precise one.

What that phrasing papers over, if you let it, is that the freedoms being cited belong in part to the same minors whose protection was the stated goal. Recast as platform capture, the ruling becomes a villain's move. Read straight, it's the Constitution doing exactly what constitutions are supposed to do: slow down a popular idea long enough to ask whether it was built right.

The lie is most dangerous not because it's crude but because it's useful. It gives Macron a political cushion — it's not our bill that failed, it's the system — while quietly lowering the standard for the rewrite. If the original ban was a rescue mission blocked by corrupt referees, then a weaker successor bill still counts as trying. That's the real payoff: not just sympathy for the government, but reduced scrutiny for whatever comes next.
