Denmark Says Russia Could Attack a NATO Country Within Months. A Fire in Poland Already Looked Like a Rehearsal.
Danish intelligence warned of a growing risk of a Russian strike on NATO soil. Hours earlier, a Polish Starlink station burned.
Truths — What Happened

The fire broke out overnight in the Masovian region of Poland, engulfing the power system and backup generator at a Starlink satellite station that helps keep Ukraine's military connected (Guardian). Deputy Prime Minister Krzysztof Gawkowski called it deliberate sabotage designed to cut internet access for institutions including Ukraine's armed forces, and said the signs point toward Russia's evolving playbook, though no one has been formally blamed (ABC). Moscow denies it, as Moscow always does.

Denmark put a number on the fear everyone in the region has been living with. Hours after the Polish fire, Danish defense intelligence chief Thomas Ahrenkiel released a report describing a "low but growing risk" that Russia will launch a limited attack on a NATO country in the coming months, using long-range drones or missiles against infrastructure that supports Ukraine, or a small unmarked troop incursion possibly disguised as protection of Russian minorities (Guardian). "The aim is not to take over the country, but to divide NATO," Ahrenkiel said (ABC). The same week, a Russian frigate fired two flares at a Danish military helicopter photographing it in international waters, one missing narrowly, and Denmark's Resilience Agency raised its threat level over expected cyberattacks (ABC).

"low but growing"
Denmark's own words for the odds of a Russian strike on NATO soil within months.

That assessment did not stay confined to Copenhagen. NATO's top commander, Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, told Reuters the alliance was stepping up intelligence-sharing in response, while insisting NATO should be "calm and confident" it can manage the risk (Reuters). Confidence is easier to state than to demonstrate. Poland scrambled fighter jets Wednesday after a Russian Mi-8 helicopter crossed from Kaliningrad into its airspace, and Romania scrambled F-16s Thursday over an unidentified target that crossed from Ukraine (Fox News). Czech President Petr Pavel, a retired general who once chaired NATO's Military Committee, said he recognizes the pattern from his years watching Russian jets test American pilots over Syria before Turkey shot one down in 2015.

There might be the time when we will have to shoot down some of these planes or unmanned vehicles that are testing our readiness.

Petr Pavel, Czech president

That threat is landing on member states with wildly different appetites to pay for it. Lithuania's president, Gitanas Nauseda, said drone incursions and sabotage have become "business as usual" on the alliance's eastern flank (Bloomberg). Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babis, who once expelled 100 Russian diplomats after the FSB was blamed for a 2014 arms depot bombing on Czech soil, is now refusing to raise defense spending past 2% next year even as his own government approved a $18 billion deficit for 2027 (DW). Poland and the Baltic states will hit 5% of GDP next year. Babis is betting his personal rapport with Donald Trump covers the gap.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The next test won't come from Moscow's air force. It will come from something smaller, deniable, and already in motion.

Poland's investigators will almost certainly stall on naming a culprit for the Starlink fire, and that silence will do Russia's work for it. Gawkowski said the signs point toward Moscow's doctrine, but attribution in sabotage cases like this typically takes months, if it comes at all; Germany still hasn't formally pinned the Leipzig/Halle drone incident on a named unit. Expect Polish officials to keep the rhetorical pressure on Russia over the next two weeks while the forensic case stays open, because the ambiguity itself is the point: NATO has to respond to hybrid attacks it can't fully prove, which is exactly the gap Denmark's intelligence chief described as Moscow's actual strategy. The loser here isn't Russia, who denies everything by habit. It's the alliance's own credibility every time it treats "many signs suggest" as a finding.

That ambiguity is about to collide with Petr Pavel's argument that ambiguity is the problem. NATO pilots will intercept another Russian aircraft or drone near alliance airspace within days, not months. Poland and Romania have already scrambled jets twice this week, and Finnair's daily GPS workarounds over the Baltic show how routine this has become. The most likely next incident will still end in an escort, not a shootdown; Grynkewich's insistence that NATO stay "calm and confident" is the position that will hold at Supreme Allied Command Europe for now. But Pavel isn't a backbencher making noise. He ran NATO's Military Committee. When a former chair starts arguing publicly for live fire, the debate has moved from theoretical to operational, and the next unresponsive Russian drone over Polish or Baltic airspace will force NATO to either shoot or explain, again, why it didn't.

80,000
The number of US troops in Europe that Gen. Grynkewich says has no "predetermined" floor, which means Washington's commitment to absorbing this risk is itself unsettled.

The country most exposed if NATO hesitates is the one least prepared to pay for readiness. Andrej Babis has bet Czechia's security on personal rapport with Trump rather than on the spending Poland and the Baltics are already meeting. The next Czech budget test comes fast: Prague's 2027 fiscal plan is already approved, and Washington's envoy has made clear the pressure won't ease. If a hybrid incident lands on Czech soil in the coming weeks, similar to the sabotage that country's own intelligence service traced to Russia a decade ago, Babis will face a choice he's currently avoiding: cash in his relationship with Trump for cover, or explain to voters why the government that approved an $18 billion deficit couldn't find room for defense. Either answer will tell the rest of NATO's laggards, Slovenia and Albania among them, whether Trump's personal loyalty is a substitute for the 3.5% target or just a story leaders tell themselves until the bill comes due.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The room NATO is standing in right now has three things in it that weren't there together a month ago: Airbus actively pitching European-built jets to a NATO member as an alternative to American ones, a NATO commander handing operational control at Norfolk to a European general for the first time, and a Czech president publicly arguing for live fire while a Danish intelligence chief puts a number on the risk. None of those three existed in combination before this week. Here's what they open.

Portugal's fighter jet decision just became a referendum on whether Europe trusts Washington to keep showing up. Airbus is pitching Eurofighter Typhoons to replace Portugal's aging F-16s, explicitly framing the plane as better suited to air policing and NATO quick-reaction missions than Lockheed's F-35 (Reuters). That pitch would have been a normal defense-industry sales pitch in any other month. It lands differently now, with Gen. Grynkewich confirming there's "no predetermined outcome" for how many US troops or capabilities stay in Europe, and with a European general taking command at Norfolk for the first time in NATO's history. Portugal doesn't have to choose the Typhoon to make a statement. The fact that Lisbon is even weighing an unsolicited European offer against the presumed American favorite, at the exact moment Washington's own commitment to European defense is described by its own commander as unsettled, is the kind of hedge militaries build quietly and governments only admit to later. If Portugal leans Typhoon, watch which other F-16 operators on NATO's edges start fielding unsolicited Airbus pitches of their own.

That hedge only matters if the readiness gap it's meant to close is real, and Petr Pavel just said, on the record, that it is.

A Czech president who once ran NATO's Military Committee just made "shoot it down" a live policy option instead of a hypothetical. Pavel's argument connects two things that were sitting separately in the room until he put them together: NATO's existing rules of engagement, which currently stop at warning and contact attempts, and Denmark's fresh intelligence assessment that Russia is likely to keep testing with exactly this kind of deniable incursion (Fox News). Grynkewich's line, that NATO has the authorities it needs "if we start to see emerging threats," was designed to sound settled. Pavel just made clear that a former chair of the Military Committee doesn't think the threshold is settled at all, he thinks it's overdue for revision. The door this opens: the next unresponsive Russian aircraft over Polish, Baltic, or Romanian airspace, and Poland and Romania have already scrambled jets twice this week, now arrives into an alliance where a credible insider has publicly pre-argued the case for firing. NATO doesn't need a new treaty or a new vote to change its posture here. It needs one incident where the political cover Pavel just supplied gets used by whichever pilot is holding the stick.

If that first shootdown happens, the second door is the one nobody in Brussels wants to walk through: what happens to the EU's industrial-policy fight once a live fire incident makes European defense spending impossible to argue against.

The "Made in Europe" fight in Brussels could get resolved by a Russian jet falling out of the sky, not by a memo. EU ministers are currently deadlocked over industrial rules for European-made defense equipment, with Spain floating compromises and allies fuming (Bloomberg). That fight has been running on its own economic logic, separate from the security debate. But a shootdown, the kind Pavel is now arguing for and NATO's posture increasingly makes plausible, would collapse the distance between those two arguments overnight. A confirmed kill changes a slow-moving procurement negotiation into an emergency one, the same way Portugal's Eurofighter pitch stops being a sales pitch and starts being a hedge the moment Washington's commitment looks uncertain. This is the generative door: Brussels has been unable to agree on industrial policy through negotiation for months, but a single incident on the eastern flank could do in a week what Spain's compromise proposals haven't managed in ministerial meetings, force EU members to actually fund the European defense-industrial base they keep saying they want, with Airbus, BAE, and Leonardo positioned to be the first winners.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Moscow's denials have a pattern of their own, and this week that pattern found a second target.

Vasile Tofan says Moldova has no plans to join NATO, and Russia is telling Moldovans he's lying. Moldova's prime minister used a Bloomberg interview to push back directly against what he described as a Kremlin narrative that his country is quietly angling for NATO membership (Bloomberg). Tofan said Moscow was promoting the idea specifically to sow fear about Moldova's pro-Western government, and he is likely right about the mechanism even if the substance of his own denial deserves scrutiny too. The claim answers a real fear for Moldovans, a small, poor country wedged between Ukraine and Romania, with a breakaway Russian-backed territory in Transnistria already inside its borders. If Moldovans believe NATO membership is imminent, that fear becomes a lever Moscow can pull whenever it wants: to justify propping up separatist authorities, to muddy Moldova's EU accession talks, or to give Russian state media a pretext for calling Chisinau a NATO proxy the same way it now calls Kyiv one. The beneficiary is specific. A frightened, divided Moldovan electorate is easier for Kremlin-aligned parties to organize than a confident one, and Moldova has a history of close, contested elections where Russian disinformation campaigns have already been documented by the country's own security services. None of that means Tofan's denial should be taken as the last word either. Governments under pressure have their own reasons to insist a door is closed even when domestic politics might one day force it open, and Tofan's declaration of permanent neutrality is a policy position dressed as a fact. The lie here is not that Moldova wants neutrality. It is Moscow's insistence that Moldova's stated neutrality is a cover story, an insistence that requires no evidence because its purpose was never to be proven. It only had to be repeated, in a region where Denmark's own intelligence service just said Russia's playbook is built entirely on manufacturing exactly this kind of doubt.
