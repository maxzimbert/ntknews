Israel Voted at a 77.7 Percent Turnout, the Highest in 27 Years. Nobody Won a Majority.
Neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's reached 61 seats; Arab parties hold the balance.
Truths — What Happened

Netanyahu Lost His Majority and Still Finished First

Pollsters had Likud losing or tied. They were wrong, and so were most of the exit polls (The Independent). With nearly 86 percent of ballots counted, Netanyahu's bloc of right-wing, far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties was on course for 53 seats and Gadi Eisenkot's opposition for 48, both short of the 61 needed in the 120-seat Knesset (BBC). The previous coalition peaked at 68 seats, and Likud appears to have slid from 32 seats to about 27 (AP).

Yet he is the one with the largest party, and he stays on as caretaker while everyone else counts. His former adviser Aviv Bushinsky called it a political resurrection. Danny Citrinowicz, a former military intelligence officer, offered a plainer reading: "He won by not losing" (Reuters). The deadliest security failure in Israel's history happened on his watch, and he kept most of his voters anyway. Haaretz editor Aluf Benn drew the sharper lesson: Israelis clung to tribal loyalty at the moment of truth instead of judging his record (Haaretz).

That left the opposition with a majority of the vote and no way to use it.

The Arab Parties Turned Out in Record Numbers, and the Opposition Has No Plan for Them

Arab turnout reached up to 80 percent in some places, against 45 to 65 percent in recent years (Al Jazeera). Bedouin voters arrived on camels, and towns opened extra polling stations to handle the lines (The Independent).

19 seats
The two main Arab parties are projected to win nearly double their current Knesset representation.

The Joint List and Ra'am are now the only route by which Eisenkot could unseat Netanyahu (Reuters). Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff, spent the campaign ruling out a government with them and on election night promised only "a stable Zionist government" (The New York Times). Yair Golan of the Democrats went the other way and urged his partners to build a change government with the Arab parties (BBC). Both offers from the Arab side were on the table within a day, from Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen and from Ra'am's Mansour Abbas, whose party sat in the 2021 coalition that lasted about 18 months (Financial Times).

Much of the turnout traces to the government itself. Arab voters cited a surge in killings by crime gangs since Itamar Ben-Gvir became national security minister, and a plan by Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich to "Judaise" Arab-majority areas (Financial Times). In Kafr Qasim, electronics-shop worker Wahby Issa said the government is encouraging racism among ordinary people. The coalition's own policies helped build the bloc that now stands in its way.

Official Results Are Days Away, and the Clock Favors Whoever Holds the Caretaker Job

Between 430,000 and 480,000 absentee ballots from soldiers and diplomats remain to be counted (BBC). In 2022, special-station votes leaned toward Netanyahu's allies (The New York Times). President Isaac Herzog expects official results next week and will then consult every faction (BBC). A designated candidate gets 28 days to build a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension, and failure can end in a dissolved Knesset and another election (Al Jazeera).

Herzog asked everyone arriving at his residence to bring an open mind and to avoid another round of voting. The opposition and the prime minister each have a reason to ignore him: one cannot reach 61 without Arab votes it has refused, and the other stays in office while the talks drag on.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

Nobody in this count has a majority, so the next two weeks are about who blinks first.

Herzog Will Almost Certainly Hand Netanyahu the First Shot at a Government, and Netanyahu Will Spend the Clock on Defectors

Official results are due Friday, and Israel's president, Isaac Herzog, will then open consultations with every faction. Likud is the largest party, which makes Netanyahu the most likely first pick (The Independent). The mandate comes with 28 days to build a coalition and a possible 14-day extension (Al Jazeera). Netanyahu has no reason to hurry. His bloc is short of 61 seats, but a stalled process leaves him as caretaker, and analysts expect a second election to strengthen him rather than change the outcome (Reuters). The most likely play is a pitch to Eisenkot's allies: join a broad government under me, or watch Arab parties become your only partner. That pitch lands hardest on Avigdor Lieberman, whose party has ruled out working with Arab factions.

The people with the most to lose from that bargain are the far-right ministers. Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich would have to be sidelined to make room for any centrist, and the arrangement is already being floated (Reuters). Expect both men to make their price public within days.

Eisenkot Will Keep Refusing Arab Partners in Public While His Camp Negotiates Outside Support in Private

Eisenkot is boxed in. He ruled out a government with Arab parties during the campaign, yet without them his bloc has no route to 61. The likely resolution is a version he can survive: no Arab ministers, no formal pact, only votes from the outside. Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen has already said his party cannot join but could prop up a minority government from outside the cabinet (Reuters). Ra'am's Mansour Abbas goes further. He says he would join, and that senior figures in Eisenkot's bloc discussed a deal with him before the election (Reuters).

The Jewish opposition may need Palestinian votes while refusing Palestinian partners. That is the central paradox.

Ameer Fakhoury, political sociologist at the Van Leer Institute

Netanyahu will answer within hours of any such leak, as he already has, by calling any reliance on Arab votes illegitimate (BBC). The cost lands on Eisenkot's right flank first, since Lieberman's party is the likeliest to walk. Abbas, meanwhile, has told Israeli television that right-wing figures have already approached him (The Atlantic). Abbas gains leverage by keeping both doors open, and he will use it until one side pays.

If Neither Side Blinks, Israel Heads Back to the Polls With Netanyahu Still in Charge

The structural outcome is the least popular and the most probable by December: failed mandates, a dissolved Knesset, and another campaign run with Netanyahu as caretaker. The opposition's hope that a rerun fixes its arithmetic is shaky, because tens of thousands of Israelis flew in to vote this time and may not return (AP). Arab turnout of up to 80 percent is also hard to repeat on demand. Netanyahu loses nothing by waiting, and Eisenkot's voters lose the patience that carried them this far.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

Three doors opened this week, and each one only exists because of what's already in the room.

Hochul's Remark Gives Eisenkot a Way to Be Rescued Without Asking

The first door is the one Eisenkot can walk through without touching it. New York Gov. Kathy Hochul told Politico Magazine her job would be "a lot easier" if Netanyahu lost, and said she cautioned Israel's government in private not to overreach (Fox News). She walked it back to NY1 as a question she was simply asked, but the sentence is now on the record. Set it beside the analyst's read that Trump could press Netanyahu toward a broader coalition and away from Ben-Gvir and Smotrich (Reuters). A week ago, Washington's leverage over Netanyahu had no domestic audience to speak to. Now it has a deadlock to work on, and a U.S. election days away in which a Democratic governor of a state with a very large Jewish population has said what her voters apparently wanted said. If Trump leans on Netanyahu, the rotation deal in The Atlantic's sketch, with Bennett eventually taking over, becomes something Eisenkot's camp could call a rescue instead of a surrender.

A Minority Government Could Make Arab Votes Count Without Arab Ministers

The second door depends on the first staying shut. If no unity deal forms, Eisenkot is left with the arithmetic problem that Fakhoury called a paradox. But two things now sit in the same room. Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen has said he would bolster a minority government from outside while refusing to join it (Reuters). And Ahmad Tibi has offered a narrower first step: Arab votes to remove Knesset speaker Amir Ohana and to block Netanyahu from leading a transitional government (Al Jazeera). Neither requires a coalition agreement, a shared platform or a single Arab minister. It requires Jewish opposition parties to vote on the same side as Arab ones on a procedural question, which is a far smaller sin than a partnership. Supporting votes on a speaker's removal could become the precedent that makes the next vote easier.

The Arab Vote Could Turn Crime Policing Into a Condition of Government

The third door is the one only the second can reveal. Once Arab votes are structurally needed even from the outside, their priorities become bargaining terms. The Joint List has named them: soaring crime, funding for Arab communities, and equality (Reuters). Most murders in Arab towns go unsolved, and critics accuse Ben-Gvir of neglecting the issue on purpose (Financial Times). A government that survives on outside Arab votes would have to answer the question Ben-Gvir's ministry never did. That is the generative version of this story: turnout that began as a protest turning into a permanent seat at the table where policing gets decided. It rests on the earlier doors opening first, and on Eisenkot choosing to count votes he has refused to name.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Two claims are doing most of the work in Israel's post-election fog, and both are built to make the voters in the middle feel they have no choice.

Netanyahu Says Any Government That Relies on Arab Votes Is "Anti-Zionist" and a Danger to Israel

Netanyahu said it at Likud headquarters on election night: "It is forbidden, simply forbidden, to endanger Israel by forming a government that relies on anti-Zionist Arab parties" (BBC). The claim answers a real fear. Many Israelis hold Oct. 7, 2023, as the reason for their distrust, and a stranger's vote in the Knesset feels like a stranger in the house.

The mechanism is simple, and it is aimed at Eisenkot's right flank. Every opposition lawmaker who might quietly accept outside Arab support now has to weigh whether Netanyahu will brand the move as treason. AP expects him to do exactly that, casting any partnership as illegitimate (AP). If the label sticks, Eisenkot's bloc has no route to 61, and Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister with a unity-government offer as the only exit.

That is who benefits. The slogan is not a security assessment. It is a lock on the opposition's only door, held by the man the opposition exists to replace.

The facts cut the other way. The Joint List's Yousef Jabareen has said his party would not join a government and would only prop one up from outside (Reuters). Ra'am's Mansour Abbas has said he would join one, and Abbas has served in one before, in 2021 under Naftali Bennett (Reuters). A party that sat in an Israeli cabinet has a hard time being "anti-Zionist" by definition.

Then there is the part Netanyahu leaves out. Abbas told Israel's Channel 12 that the right wing has already approached him about working together (The Atlantic). The same Arab votes that endanger Israel when they back Eisenkot become a negotiating partner when they might back Likud. Netanyahu courted Arab parties in 2021 as well (The Atlantic). The objection was never to Arab votes. It was to whose coalition they landed in.

Ameer Fakhoury of the Van Leer Institute named the trap the slogan feeds on: "The Jewish opposition may need Palestinian votes while refusing Palestinian partners" (Reuters). Netanyahu has made that refusal feel like patriotism. It keeps the one parliamentary majority against him from ever becoming a government.

Arab citizens are about a fifth of Israel's population (Reuters). Many of them turned out for the first time in memory because they had been told they were a threat. They are now being told that the threat is their vote.
