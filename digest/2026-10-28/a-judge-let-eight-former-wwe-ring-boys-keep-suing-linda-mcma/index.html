A federal judge kept the "ring boys" abuse suit alive against Education Secretary McMahon.
Truths — What Happened

A Federal Judge Refused to Dismiss the Case Against Linda McMahon and WWE

Eight men, identified in court papers only as John Doe 1 through 8, say they were teenagers when WWE recruited them as "ring boys," part-time crew who ran errands and set up events. They allege that ring announcer Mel Phillips and two other officials abused them in the 1980s and 1990s, and that Vince and Linda McMahon should have stopped it. Phillips and the other two alleged abusers died years before the suit was filed. The McMahons and WWE asked a federal court to throw the case out.

On Oct. 19, Judge James Bredar in Baltimore said no. The defendants argued that a 2023 Maryland law, which erased the time limit on child sex abuse claims, was unconstitutional and left them unable to mount a defense decades later. Bredar rejected both arguments (Reuters). Plaintiffs, he wrote, can still produce documents to dispute and witnesses to cross-examine. The defendants had also asked the court to name the eight men publicly. Bredar declined, citing the risk of "retraumatization" (The 74).

The ruling carries a practical consequence. A scheduling call is set for Nov. 24, and the schedule could include deposing McMahon about what she knew while she ran WWE alongside her husband from 1980 until 2009 (Inside Higher Ed). McMahon has said in court filings that she never supervised Phillips and knew nothing of his actions. Her lawyer, Laura Brevetti, has called the suit "filled with scurrilous lies, exaggerations and misrepresentations."

29 investigations from 200+ complaints
The Office for Civil Rights has opened cases on about one in seven sexual violence complaints since January 2025.

The Department She Runs Is Being Asked to Do What the Plaintiffs Say WWE Didn't

That timing makes the case harder for McMahon to keep at arm's length. In July she announced investigations into 20 school districts over how they handle staff-on-student sexual misconduct. "Parents should never have to wonder whether their kids' school employs and protects sexual predators," she said. In early October her department widened that effort to more states and the New York City Department of Education (The 74).

Advocates say the enforcement record looks different. A ProPublica analysis found the Office for Civil Rights received more than 200 sexual violence complaints under the current administration and opened investigations into 29. In an Oct. 20 letter to Congress, civil rights groups wrote that the department has reached one Title IX resolution agreement involving sexual violence in nearly two years. The office is also being moved to the Justice Department as part of the plan to phase out the agency.

The rules those complaints are judged under changed too. McMahon's department formally rescinded the 2024 Title IX regulations and reinstated the 2020 version, which narrows the definition of sexual harassment and gives more due process rights to the accused (The Week). The National Women's Law Center said survivors' needs were ignored.

The week the Times reported on her $43,000 furniture purchase, she was still defending the closure of the department that buys it (New York Times). Her confirmation hearing last year included a direct question from Sen. Tammy Baldwin about whether survivors could trust her. Her answer came with a pledge. The deposition schedule, if it comes, will test it.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The next fourteen days belong to a Nov. 24 phone call and a midterm calendar, and both lead to the same witness chair.

The Scheduling Call Will Put McMahon's Deposition on the Table, and Her Lawyers Will Fight the Date

The Nov. 24 conference is where Judge Bredar sets the pace, and the plaintiffs' lawyers at DiCello Levitt will ask for McMahon's testimony early. Her side will almost certainly counter with a longer discovery window, a narrower scope, or an argument that a sitting cabinet secretary cannot be pulled away from her duties. Expect that last one in particular. Defendants who lose a motion to dismiss rarely stop fighting; they move the fight to the calendar. The most likely outcome is a schedule that pushes any deposition into 2027, which happens to land after the midterms.

That timing matters to the eight men, who have waited decades and now wait on a docket. It matters to McMahon because delay is her cheapest defense. She has said in court filings that she never supervised Mel Phillips, and a sworn answer under cross-examination is a different instrument than a filing signed by a lawyer. Every month of delay is a month she is not asked what "open secret" meant inside her own company.

The Same Facts Will Become Campaign Material Before Any Testimony Exists

Delay in court does not mean quiet in politics. Democrats are weeks from an election they believe they can win in the Senate, and committee staff are already ranking targets. Senator Maggie Hassan's team and Representative Robert Garcia's whiteboard list corruption, immigration enforcement and the Iran war, with the Education Department's dismantling a natural addition (The Atlantic). The furniture purchase and the lawsuit hand them a tidy pairing: a secretary spending $43,000 on her office while closing the department, and defending herself against abuse claims while her agency polices others for the same failure.

Look for Democratic candidates to use the pairing in the final stretch, and for Sen. Tammy Baldwin to revisit the confirmation exchange where McMahon pledged to protect survivors. If Democrats win a chamber, an oversight letter to the department about its sexual violence complaint backlog is a low-cost first move, because it needs no subpoena and no cooperation from Justice to generate a headline.

The Real Test Comes When Complaints Move to the Justice Department

The structural exposure sits beneath both stories. As the Office for Civil Rights transfers to Justice, advocates will track whether the caseload shrinks further, and the likeliest result is that it does. A civil rights office folded into a department that Trump has controlled completely, per The Atlantic, answers to a different boss and a different set of priorities.

The loser in that scenario is the student who filed a complaint and waits. The winner, narrowly, is any official who prefers that the question of who failed whom stay in a filing cabinet. McMahon's lawsuit, meanwhile, keeps the question open in public, under oath, with a judge who has already told her the defense is overblown.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

Three doors this week's rulings opened, and each one leads to a room the case alone could not reach.

A Maryland Law Other States Struck Down Now Has a Federal Judge Behind It

Here is the combination that did not exist in September. The top courts in Maine, Utah and Kentucky have ruled that reviving expired abuse claims is invalid, while the Maryland Supreme Court upheld its own state's version last year. Judge Bredar has now added a federal ruling that the same law survives the Constitution's ban on retroactive punishment and its due process guarantee (Reuters). That leaves a split between state high courts and a federal trial judge, and defendants with Paul Weiss on retainer are the kind who appeal. An appeal to the Fourth Circuit would take a Baltimore dispute over teenage crew members and turn it into the test case for every post-#MeToo revival law in the country. The McMahons are the unlikely authors of that precedent: the more money they spend fighting this, the more likely a higher court settles whether decades-old claims can be revived at all. Delay is cheap for them. A published appellate opinion, win or lose, is not.

An Appeal Would Open a Second Door: A Sitting Cabinet Secretary Asking Courts to Shield Her From Survivors

Enter the room the first door reveals. If the appeal comes, McMahon's argument that she cannot be spared for a deposition meets her department's own record in the same news cycle. The Office for Civil Rights is moving to Justice, and the complaint backlog is now a public number advocates are tracking. A defense strategy built on "unfair to defend old claims" sits awkwardly beside an agency that tells survivors to wait. Reporters and Senate staff would not need new facts to write that story. They would only need the docket and the complaint log side by side.

The Generative Door: Survivors' Lawyers and Oversight Staff Now Share a Calendar

Not every room is worse. Representative Robert Garcia's staff and Senator Maggie Hassan's team spent the fall building witness lists and draft information requests, and they have never had a live civil case to read alongside them (The Atlantic). DiCello Levitt gets discovery powers that Congress lacks; Congress gets a subpoena threat that private lawyers lack. The reform version of this overlap is narrow and concrete: a bipartisan bill that requires the Office for Civil Rights to publish how many sexual violence complaints it opens and closes each quarter. It costs no money, needs no cooperation from Justice, and gives the 200-plus complainants something no one has offered them yet, which is a count. Republicans who campaigned on protecting children from predators in schools would have to explain a no vote on a spreadsheet.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Two sentences are doing the heaviest lifting in McMahon's defense, and one of them is hers.

"Plaintiffs Can't Get a Fair Trial Because the Witnesses Are Dead"

The argument runs through WWE's filings, and McMahon's lawyers signed onto it. The Maryland law is unconstitutional, they told Judge Bredar, because Mel Phillips and the two other alleged abusers died years before anyone sued, memories have faded, and evidence has vanished. Defending a claim from the 1980s, they said, is difficult or impossible. It is an appealing argument because it borrows a principle most people hold: no one should lose a case to a ghost.

Look at who is pleading unfairness. The people who say they were harmed as teenagers waited decades, and the defendants are the company and executives whose own records, personnel files and former colleagues are the evidence in question. Dead witnesses cut both ways. The accused can no longer be cross-examined, but they can no longer be deposed either, and a company that fired Phillips in the early 1990s presumably recorded why. The plaintiffs claim the McMahons knew. The proof of that, or its absence, sits in paper and in the memories of living people, including the defendant who is now a cabinet secretary.

Who benefits when the argument spreads? Not the dead men. The real beneficiaries are defendants in every comparable suit, whose lawyers want courts to treat the passage of time as an acquittal. Delay is the product being sold, and the buyers include any institution with an old file and a good law firm.

The judge heard the argument and measured it against how these cases actually work. The concerns, he found, were overblown. His reasoning was ordinary: plaintiffs still have to produce documents and witnesses, and defendants still get to cross-examine them. A statute of limitations is a rule about deadlines. It is not a finding that nothing happened.

The second sentence is the one McMahon offered at her confirmation hearing, when Sen. Tammy Baldwin asked whether survivors could trust her. She promised to "uphold and protect those investigations to make sure that those students are treated fairly on both sides." The phrase "both sides" does quiet work. It places a child who reports abuse and an adult accused of it on the same scale, and it describes the posture her lawyers have taken in Baltimore: the burden of time belongs to the accused. A department that shares that posture will treat every old complaint as a fairness problem for the respondent first.

States are not united on this. Maine, Utah and Kentucky have struck down similar laws, so the dead-witness argument has won before. That is why this is a dispute over law that deserves argument in open court. What deserves scrutiny is the claim that unfairness only runs one direction. A judge who weighed both found it did not.
