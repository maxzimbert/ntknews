Diesel Hit $6.53 a Gallon This Year. Trump's Fix Is a Farm Tax Break He Can't Legally Promise Alone.
Trump blamed Ukraine and California for gas prices. Diesel still costs $139 more a month.
Truths — What Happened

Diesel drivers are paying $139.32 more a month than they were in October 2025, and gasoline drivers are paying $66.86 more (The Independent). Trump's explanation, posted to Truth Social on Monday, skipped past the war he started in February and landed on Ukraine and "Blue States, like California" (Al Jazeera). The math doesn't hold. Oil flow through the Strait of Hormuz had largely returned to pre-war levels by September, and before 2022 the U.S. imported only about 8% of its petroleum products from Russia anyway (The New Republic; The Independent). Most U.S. refining capacity sits in Texas and Louisiana, states Republicans run, not California (The Independent).

That math problem landed in Nebraska on Monday, where Trump arrived to announce an executive order loosening limits on red dye diesel, the tax-exempt fuel farmers use off-road (Financial Times). The order is also expected to push the Department of Transportation toward waiving road diesel taxes with the states (Reuters). Texas Gov. Greg Abbott had already signed a 30-day order doing something similar last week, which is the tell here: when a crisis forces two Republican executives to improvise around federal fuel tax law one state at a time, the crisis arrived before the policy did (Financial Times).

Nebraska wasn't the only stop on the tour. In Alaska, Vice President JD Vance campaigned Monday for Sen. Dan Sullivan, trailing Democrat Mary Peltola by 7 points in a New York Times poll, carrying a $150 million Energy Department check for 223 miles of power line (Reuters). Sullivan has publicly broken with Trump on one point, calling for a ban on diesel exports the White House considered and dropped after oil executives lobbied against it and G7 allies agreed to release 100 million barrels from reserves instead (Financial Times).

None of it has caught up with the politics yet. A recent AP-NORC poll found 65% of Americans blame Trump's own policies for higher prices, and in Mesa, Arizona, 2024 Trump voter Dan Lloyd told the AP he's tired of picking up the tab (Al Jazeera; The Independent).

He hurt himself stepping into this. The American people eat it every time, whether it's interest rates, food, gasoline.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The red diesel order buys Trump maybe three weeks before the next number lands on a farmer's fuel receipt and the politics catch up anyway.

The Nebraska order will hit legal challenges before it hits a single fuel pump. An executive order waiving federal diesel tax law requires either statutory authority or a genuine emergency declaration, and loosening red dye diesel rules this broadly looks more like the former stretched to cover the latter (Reuters). The most likely outcome in the next two weeks is a lawsuit from a state attorney general, probably a Democratic one in California, arguing the administration can't unilaterally waive a tax Congress wrote into law. Watch whether the Department of Transportation actually issues guidance to states this week or simply announces intent and stalls, the same pattern that left Abbott's Texas order as a 30-day stopgap rather than a federal fix.

That stall matters more in Alaska than it does in Nebraska. Sullivan has already broken with the White House once, calling for the export ban Trump dropped under industry pressure, and a diesel order that arrives as announcement rather than relief gives him nothing to run on against Peltola's 7-point lead before Election Day. The most likely next step is Sullivan distancing himself further in the next 10 days, not embracing the Nebraska order but pointing back to his own export-ban position as evidence he saw the crisis coming before the White House did. That's a Republican senator in a competitive race building daylight between himself and a president whose energy policy keeps landing as a half-measure.

The deeper problem is the one neither order touches: diesel prices track Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining capacity and Iranian pressure on the Strait of Hormuz, not U.S. tax policy. Ukraine has no incentive to stop hitting refineries while the war continues, and Trump's Sept. 13 request that Kyiv ease off went nowhere. The most probable path through late October is prices staying near record highs regardless of what Washington does domestically, which means the administration will almost certainly roll out a second and then a third targeted relief measure, state waivers, reserve releases, something aimed at truckers specifically, each one arriving a news cycle after the last price spike rather than ahead of it. That sequencing is the real risk for Republicans in competitive seats: voters watching a White House visibly chase a problem it insists isn't its fault will draw their own conclusion well before any executive order reaches a gas station.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The Nebraska visit opened one door and the Alaska trip opened a second, and together they point somewhere neither state's race can reach on its own.

A Sullivan break from Trump becomes the model other Senate Republicans copy before Nov. 3. Sullivan calling for an export ban Trump already rejected is a data point right now. It becomes a strategy the moment one more Republican in a competitive race does the same thing, and the combination that makes this newly reachable is specific: a sitting senator has already broken publicly with a sitting president from his own party, on the exact issue voters say they blame the president for, a month before the election. That precedent didn't exist last week. Watch Maine's Susan Collins or Ohio's Bernie Moreno-adjacent races for the same move: distance from the White House on energy policy specifically, paired with a local fix, Collins has already leaned on LNG terminal money in her state before. If a second Senate Republican does what Sullivan did, reporters will start asking White House press secretaries to name every Republican who hasn't, which turns a single senator's maneuver into a defection story the administration can't contain state by state.

That first door, a visible Senate Republican break, is what makes the second one possible. A state attorney general lawsuit against the red diesel order becomes the vehicle for a broader challenge to executive energy emergency powers. The Probabilities section already flagged the legal exposure. What's newly combinable is who's positioned to bring that suit and what else they could attach to it. California's Rob Bonta has sued the Trump administration on energy and environmental grounds multiple times this year, and a red diesel challenge gives him a plaintiff with standing, lost tax revenue, and a sympathetic argument, California refineries closing, that Trump has already handed him by blaming the state by name. If Bonta or a similar Democratic AG files, the suit isn't just about diesel tax law. It becomes a vehicle for the bigger question neither Truths nor Probabilities raised: whether a president can invoke emergency authority to waive federal tax statutes for political timing rather than genuine emergency, a question that outlasts this specific order and this specific election.

The third door is the one worth naming because it doesn't require anything to go wrong. The G7 reserve release becomes the template for a standing allied fuel-crisis mechanism. A hundred million barrels moved once, under pressure, as a one-time fix. But the infrastructure that moved them, the coordination between Washington and European governments, the pressure campaign that got France and Germany to release stockpiles, now exists and didn't a month ago. If diesel prices spike again before Election Day, which Probabilities already expects, the G7 has a tested playbook instead of a blank page. That's the generative version of this story: the same crisis that's exposing Trump's legal overreach at home is also building the first real precedent for allied energy-crisis coordination outside OPEC's control, a door nobody walked through on purpose.

Lies / Narrative Distortions

Trump told Americans their gas problem has a villain, and he picked the two with no way to answer back.

Ukraine and California are closing refineries to make gas expensive. Trump made this claim Monday on Truth Social, pointing at Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and "Dumocrats" in "Blue States, like California" as the real reason pump prices hit $4.36 a gallon (Al Jazeera). It's an easy claim to believe if you're a Nebraska farmer watching your diesel bill double and you want a villain who isn't the man standing in your state announcing a fix. Ukraine strikes Russian refineries because Russia invaded Ukraine, not because Kyiv wants Americans paying more at the pump, and the New Republic noted the obvious fix sitting unmentioned in Trump's post: Ukraine would likely stop hitting Russian refineries the day Putin stopped invading it (The New Republic). The California accusation fares worse. The state did lose two refineries and about 17% of its refining capacity over the past year, but Trump offered no account of why a Democratic governor would want to shut down in-state production and hand Republicans a talking point a month before the midterms, and the Independent couldn't find one either, calling the claim's logic "not entirely clear" (The Independent). The refining capacity Trump needs Americans not to think about sits in Texas and Louisiana, states run by his own party.

The person who benefits from this claim spreading is Trump himself, with a specific and narrow use for it: a month before Republicans risk the Senate, "the war I started" is a worse answer than "Democrats did this to you." Every dollar he reroutes onto Kyiv and Sacramento is a dollar that doesn't land on the Iran war's opening months, when the Strait of Hormuz shutdown first spiked the price he's now claiming has nothing to do with it. Reuters flagged the same contradiction, noting Trump made the claim even as his own administration scrambled for a G7 reserve release to cover the shortfall his war created (Reuters). That's the mechanism underneath the message: a president does not spend a week brokering an emergency diesel order and an allied stockpile release over a crisis he believes someone else caused. He does that when the bill has his own name on it, and he's betting voters won't check the invoice before Nov. 3.
