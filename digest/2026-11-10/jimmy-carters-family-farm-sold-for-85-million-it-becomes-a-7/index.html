Jimmy Carter's Family Farm Sold for $8.5 Million. It Becomes a 7,000-Acre Public Hunting Ground.
The Conservation Fund bought Carter's 2,500-acre Georgia farm to open it to the public.
Truths — What Happened

The Conservation Fund Bought the Land Carter Worked as a Teenager and Returned To at 100

Jimmy Carter spent most of his life within walking distance of the same fields. He worked them beside his father, came back in 1953 after the Navy to run the family business when his father died, and kept visiting until he was physically unable to, at which point he sometimes found someone to drive him through them (Washington Post). He died in late 2024 at age 100. Last week, the Conservation Fund announced it had bought the farm for about $8.5 million, roughly 2,458 acres outside Plains (The Guardian; FOX 5 Atlanta).

Amy Carter, the former president's only daughter, said she and her three brothers decided quickly that the sale felt right. The land passes to a buyer whose stated goal is to keep it from being sold again.

The Secret Service used to stay out of sight when Carter visited, Amy Carter recalled, so he could feel alone. That solitude is the part the sale gives up. The land will no longer be anyone's private retreat.

The Plan Turns a Family Farm Into Hunting and Fishing Ground by 2027

The nonprofit intends to open the property for hunting, fishing and other outdoor recreation as soon as 2027, then transfer it to Georgia's natural resources department in 2028 (The Guardian). Georgia would fold it into the Lanahassee Creek Wildlife Management Area, growing that area to nearly 7,000 acres. Stacy Funderburke, the Conservation Fund's vice president for the central Southeast region, said the land would be protected "in the way he would have wanted to see," and open to others who want the same connection to it (Washington Post).

The habitat is the practical payoff. The fields and forests hold longleaf pine and threatened gopher tortoises, along with birds and other wildlife. Protecting them is the part of the deal that outlasts the ceremony.

Carter's own hunting habits were modest: turkey season every spring, according to Blake Sullivan, who managed the farm and timberland for more than four decades. Sullivan said Carter "saw his job as being a good steward," and called the sale "a fitting tribute."

They liked to go and walk the land. That was their escape, to get out into the woods.

Blake Sullivan, longtime manager of Carter's farm

It Fits a Record Carter Built in Washington

The farm is not the only Carter site in Plains. The Jimmy Carter National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service, covers his boyhood home and the site of his 1976 campaign headquarters, and the sale does not touch it (Washington Post). The two properties now split a legacy: the federal government keeps the buildings, and the state will keep the land.

The Conservation Fund drew a straight line from the farm to what Carter did as president. He signed the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act in late 1980, protecting more than 104 million acres, still the largest expansion of protected public land in U.S. history (The Guardian). He also established 39 national park system units, among them the Martin Luther King Jr. National Historic Site and the Chattahoochee River National Recreation Area in Atlanta.

The farm that raised him will now answer to the public, with a 2027 opening date and a 2028 handoff to Georgia.

Probabilities — What Will Likely Happen

The deal is signed. What happens to the land over the next two years depends on who has to say yes.

Georgia Will Almost Certainly Accept the Land, and Name It After Carter

A free 2,500-acre gift that expands an existing wildlife area is the easiest vote a state agency ever takes. Georgia's natural resources department has no reason to refuse, and the Conservation Fund has said the transfer is planned for 2028. The first public sign will likely be a joint announcement or a memorandum between the nonprofit and the state, probably framed around the Carter name, since the name is what makes a land transfer a news event. Politicians in a state that voted Carter in, and later voted against his party, will want their photograph at the gate.

The winners are the officials who get credit without writing a check. The loser is any rival claim on the land, because once the state holds title, the Conservation Fund's promise that the property stays protected becomes a matter of state law rather than a nonprofit's good intentions.

The Hard Part Is the Stretch Between Owners, Not the Deal Itself

The Conservation Fund will hold the farm for roughly two years before the state takes it. That gap is where these projects usually get tested. Someone has to pay the property taxes, fence the boundaries, manage the longleaf pine and keep out trespassers who have heard the Carter name and assume the gates are already open. Someone also has to decide what happens to the working farmland, which has been tilled for generations and does not stay a field without a farmer.

The most likely outcome is a long list of small decisions made quietly by Funderburke's regional team: whether to lease the open fields to local growers, whether to burn the pine understory on a schedule that protects the gopher tortoises, whether to open limited access before 2027. Watch the Conservation Fund's regional announcements and Georgia's hunting regulations for the first draft of those rules. A proposed season or permit requirement for the new acreage will show how public "public" will actually be.

The Carter Name Will Draw Visitors Faster Than the Land Can Absorb Them

The Jimmy Carter National Historical Park already sits a short drive from the farm, and anyone who tours the boyhood home will now ask where the fields are. Plains is a small town with limited parking and few places to eat, and a hunting and fishing area is not built for a tour bus. The most probable result is pressure on the town before the state is ready: visitors showing up at the farm gate, local businesses asking the state for signage, and park staff fielding questions about land they do not manage.

Amy Carter said the Secret Service once stayed out of sight so her father could feel alone. The next two years will test whether anyone can build a public place that keeps some of that quiet. Georgia gets one chance to decide whether this is a destination or a preserve.

Possibilities — What Could Happen

The deal is done and the land is headed to the state. These are the doors it opened that nobody had walked through before.

A Working Farm Inside a Wildlife Area Could Become a Template for Georgia's Row-Crop Land

The Conservation Fund inherited more than woods. Carter's family grew peanuts, corn and sugar cane on this ground, and a working farm does not stay a field without a farmer. Until this week, the choice for a landowner like this was binary: sell to a developer or an agribusiness, or put a conservation easement on the land and keep farming it privately. Now a third combination exists. A nonprofit with an 8.5 million-dollar balance sheet commitment, a state agency with a wildlife area next door and a famous name that draws attention are all in the same room. Blake Sullivan has managed this land for more than four decades, and his knowledge is the missing piece. If the fund leases the open fields to local growers under wildlife-friendly terms, Georgia gets a public hunting area with a farm built into it, and the next family weighing an offer on its own acreage can point to the Carter parcel as proof that the third option exists.

The Carter Name Could Pull Private Money Toward Neighboring Landowners

This door depends on the first. Once the farm sits inside an expanded wildlife area, every parcel along its border looks different to the Conservation Fund. A buyer that has just assembled one tract now holds the relationships, the appraisals and the donor attention to assemble the next. The Carter story gave the fund something it rarely has: a reason for a stranger to write a check. If the nonprofit uses that attention to approach neighbors, the 7,000-acre target stops being a ceiling and becomes a starting point. The losers are the speculators who assumed small tracts near Plains would trickle onto the market one at a time.

The Gap Between Owners Could Become Georgia's First Test of Phased Public Access

The two-year hold before the 2028 transfer was meant to be a holding pattern. It could instead become a pilot. Because the Conservation Fund still owns the land when it first opens in 2027, it can set the rules before the state inherits them: limited permits, seasonal closures, a visitor cap tuned to what Plains can absorb. The Jimmy Carter National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service, already draws people to town, so the fund can watch real crowd data before the state writes its regulations. If it records what works, Georgia's natural resources department receives the land with a tested playbook rather than a guess. That is the generative outcome: a handoff that arrives with its lessons attached.
